Eight million dollars of patience

A gas station attendant who became a janitor died in Vermont with nearly eight million dollars, and almost nobody knew. By the end of this page you will be able to explain exactly where that money came from, and why the answer is the opposite of how day trading works. You will be quizzed the whole way.

[[CH open|A box of paper in Brattleboro]] [[STEP will|The will]]

Ronald Read was born in 1921 in Dummerston, a small town in southern Vermont. He walked or hitchhiked about four miles to get to high school, and in 1940 he became the first person in his family to graduate. He served in World War II and came home at the end of 1945.

Then he worked. For about 25 years he pumped gas and fixed cars at Haviland's service station in Brattleboro. He and his older brother later bought the station, and they sold it when he retired in 1979. Retirement lasted about a year. He then spent 17 years as a part time janitor at the local J. C. Penney store, until 1997.

He was famously careful with money. People remembered a fraying jacket he kept wearing by holding it together with a safety pin, a secondhand car, and a habit of parking far away to avoid paying the meter. He chopped his own firewood and picked up fallen branches for his wood stove. Few people had any hint. The clues were in what he read, The Wall Street Journal, Barron's, which is a weekly investing magazine, and books from the public library.

He died in June 2014, at 92. In February 2015 his will was made public, and the town found out. He left $4.8 million to Brattleboro Memorial Hospital and $1.2 million to Brooks Memorial Library, the largest gifts either had ever received. His whole estate came to nearly $8 million.

Here is his life on one line. Every date comes from news reports of his will and his obituary.

[[FIG life|Ronald Read, 1921 to 2014. The purple dot is his earliest reported stock purchase. The gray dot is Lehman Brothers, a company he owned whose shares became worthless. The filled dot is the money he gave away.]] [[STEP box|What was in the box]]

A few weeks later The Wall Street Journal looked at what he had owned. Almost all of the money was in shares of companies, small pieces of ownership that the next chapter builds from scratch. Owning shares of a company is also called owning its stock. When he died he owned stock in at least 95 different companies, many of them held for decades. Among them were Procter and Gamble, which makes Tide and Crest, the bank JPMorgan Chase, General Electric, Johnson and Johnson, Dow Chemical, the jam maker J. M. Smucker, and the drugstore chain CVS.

He kept the paper certificates that proved his ownership in a safe deposit box at his bank. Stacked up, they were about five inches thick. He avoided technology companies and anything fashionable. And when his companies paid him cash, he generally used it to buy more of their shares.

He was not perfect at it. One of his companies was Lehman Brothers, the bank that collapsed in 2008. Its shares became essentially worthless. You will see in [[C baskets]] why that barely mattered to him.

[[STEP question|The one question]]

Put this next to what researchers have found about day trading. One of the largest studies used every trade on the Taiwan Stock Exchange from 1992 to 2006. Fewer than 1 in 100 day traders could beat the market after fees, reliably, from one year to the next. And the trading track showed that in futures like ES, every point one trader wins, another loses.

Ronald Read never traded fast, never had special information, and never earned a large salary. Yet he ended up with nearly $8 million.

So this whole page answers one question. Where did his money come from, and who lost what he won?

The second half of that question has a surprising answer, and [[C sum]] gives it. First, a quick check of where your picture starts.

Why one man proves nothing

You hear about Ronald Read because his story is rare and surprising. Nobody writes about the frugal janitor who saved for fifty years and ended with an ordinary amount. So this page does not treat him as proof of anything. It uses him as a puzzle, and then checks his result against arithmetic and against a century of market data.

Start with something small. Picture a late night ice cream shop owned by a few partners. The shop has freezers, a lease, a brand, and customers. Each year, after paying for ingredients, staff, rent and taxes, it has money left over. That leftover money is the profit, also called earnings.

The partners need a clear way to say who owns how much. So they cut the ownership into equal pieces, say 1,000 of them. Each piece is called a share. The total number of shares that exist is called the shares outstanding. If you own 50 of the 1,000 shares, you own 50 ÷ 1,000 = 5% of the shop. Loosely, that is five percent of the freezers, the brand, and every dollar of profit. [[S promise]] makes that sentence precise.

The picture below shows the same thing. Each block is 50 shares. Purple is your slice. The pale blocks belong to everyone else.

[[FIG slices|A company divided into 1,000 equal shares. Purple is the slice one owner holds.]]

Notice what a share is not. It is not a bet on the next price, and it is not a loan. It is a fraction of a real business, and it keeps that meaning whether or not anyone ever trades it.

[[STEP public|The same idea, very large]]

A public company is one whose shares anyone can buy and sell on a stock exchange. It works exactly like the shop, just bigger. Procter and Gamble is cut into billions of shares instead of 1,000. The company does not sell them to you directly. Owners who want to sell meet buyers who want to buy in the same kind of order book you studied in Trading Week 1, and the price of a share is just the last trade.

One difference from ES. ES has a single book in Aurora. A US stock trades at the same time on more than a dozen exchanges, each with its own book of the same kind, and a large share of trades happen off the exchanges altogether. Most orders sent from phone apps are filled by big trading firms called wholesalers. The price you see is the last trade reported from any of these places.

The paper certificates in Ronald Read's box were the old way to prove ownership. Today almost nobody holds paper. Your broker keeps an electronic record that you own the shares, and the meaning is identical.

[[STEP cap|What the market charges for the whole company]]

If you know the price of one share and the number of shares outstanding, you can work out what the market is charging for the entire company. Multiply them. The result is called the market capitalization, usually shortened to market cap.

Say a company has 2 billion shares outstanding and the last trade was $50. Its market cap is 2 billion × $50 = $100 billion.

Be careful with the word. Market cap is a price, not a value. Trading Week 1 showed that a price is only the last agreement between two orders. Market cap just multiplies that last agreement by the number of shares. If the price falls 10% in a nervous afternoon, the market cap falls 10% too, even though the factories and customers are exactly the same.

[[STEP eps|Your slice of the profit]]

The most useful single number for an owner is the profit that belongs to one share. Take the company's earnings for a year and divide by the shares outstanding. That is earnings per share, written EPS.

A company that earns $6 billion with 2 billion shares outstanding has EPS of $6 billion ÷ 2 billion = $3. If you own 100 shares, $300 of that year's profit belongs to your shares, whether the company hands it to you or keeps it in the business. The next chapter is about which of those happens.

[[STEP promise|What the share actually promises you]]

A share does not let you walk into the shop and take five percent of the freezers. Legally the company owns the freezers, and you own a piece of the company. That piece gives you three rights.

First, you get what is left over. Customers pay the company. Out of that money it pays suppliers, staff, rent, interest to anyone who lent it money, and taxes. Whatever remains is the profit, and only the owners have a claim on it. That is why owners are called residual claimants. Residual means left over.

Flip between the two years below. Only the sales change. Read each row from the top, and watch the bottom row, which is what reaches the owners.

[[TOGGLE paid]]

Here is what just happened. In the good year the owners got the last $20. In the bad year everyone ahead of them was still owed the same, so nothing was left and the owners got nothing for that year. The lenders were still paid, out of the company's savings or new borrowing.

Second, you get a vote. Owners elect a board of directors, usually one vote per share. The board hires the managers and decides how much of the profit to pay out and how much to keep. You cannot demand a dividend. You can only vote for the people who decide.

Third, you can never lose more than you paid. If the company collapses owing billions, its lenders cannot come after you for the rest. That protection is called limited liability. It is a real difference from ES, where a bad enough move can cost you more than the money in your account. Trading Week 2 shows how.

Every year a profitable company has the same decision to make. Its profit belongs to its owners, but the board decides how to deliver it. There are three main ways.

It can pay some out in cash. It can use some to buy back its own shares. And it can keep the rest, to reinvest in the business by building new factories, hiring, or buying other companies, or to pay down what it owes. Here is one made up year.

[[FIG split|One year of profit split three ways. Teal is cash paid to owners, blue is the company buying its own shares, green is profit kept and reinvested.]]

Each path puts value in the owners' hands in a different way. The next three steps take them one at a time.

[[STEP dividends|Dividends, the cash path]]

Cash paid out to owners is called a dividend. It is set as an amount per share and usually paid every three months. If a company pays $1 per share each quarter and you own 100 shares, you receive $100 four times a year.

To compare dividends across companies with very different share prices, people divide the yearly dividend by the price. That is the dividend yield. A share at $50 that pays $2 a year has a yield of $2 ÷ $50 = 4%.

Some companies have paid for a very long time. Procter and Gamble, one of Ronald Read's companies, has paid a dividend every year since it became a corporation in 1890, and in April 2026 it raised the amount for the 70th year in a row.

Keep these two apart

A dividend is not free extra money on top of the share. Each dividend has a cutoff day, called the ex dividend date. Anyone who buys the share on or after that day does not get the coming payment. On that day the share price typically drops by about the dividend amount, because the cash is about to leave the company. The dividend is your profit being delivered, not a gift.

[[STEP buyback|Your turn. Buybacks, the invisible path]]

The second path is quieter. A company can use its cash to buy its own shares from owners who want to sell, and then cancel them. That is a buyback. Step through what it does to an owner who does nothing. Watch the purple block, the cash box on the right, and the line at the bottom.

[[STEPPER buyback]]

Here is what just happened. The owners who sold received cash, just like a dividend, but only the ones who wanted it. Everyone who stayed now owns a bigger fraction of the same profit. Earnings per share rose from $10 to $12.50 even though the company earned exactly the same amount.

Now look at what it cost. The cash that paid the sellers was the company's cash, and a tenth of it was yours. You now own a bigger slice of a company that has less cash in it. If the company paid a fair price, those two changes cancel exactly, the same way a share price drops by the dividend on the ex dividend date. So a buyback is not new money. It is a second way of handing over profit the company already earned. It helps the owners who stay only when the company buys its shares for less than they are really worth, and it hurts them when it overpays.

This matters when you read company reports. A rising EPS can mean the business is growing, or it can mean the same profit is being divided among fewer shares. Only the first one makes the owners richer.

[[STEP reinvest|Reinvestment, the slow path]]

The third path is the one you cannot see at all in the short run. Profit the company keeps is used to build the business. A drugstore chain opens new stores. A consumer goods company builds a new factory. If those projects earn more than the owners could have earned by putting the same money somewhere else, the company's future profits are larger, and so is each owner's slice of them.

This is where most of the long run growth in earnings comes from. It is also where things can go wrong. A company that wastes its kept profit on projects that earn too little makes its owners poorer. Judging that is part of what Investing Week 4 is about.

Notice what all three paths have in common. The money comes from customers buying the company's products, and it flows to the owners. Nobody on the other side of a trade had to lose it.

If a share is a slice of a business, why does its price jump around every single day while the business barely changes?

You already know the answer from Trading Week 1. The price is only the last trade. Each day it is pushed around by who happens to be in a hurry, by news, by rumors, by big investors adjusting what they hold, and by moods. None of that changes how many tubes of toothpaste a company sells this month.

So over days and weeks, most of what you see on a stock chart is noise, movement that tells you almost nothing about the business underneath.

[[STEP pe|The price to earnings ratio]]

To connect price and business, compare them directly. Divide the share price by the earnings per share. The result is the price to earnings ratio, written P/E. P/E is also called the multiple, because it says how many times the earnings the market pays.

A share at $60 with EPS of $3 has a P/E of 20. One way to read it is that the market is charging 20 years of this year's earnings for a slice of the company. A P/E of 10 means it is charging 10 years' worth.

Flip the definition around and you get the most useful sentence in this chapter. Price = EPS × P/E. The price can change for only two reasons. Either the earnings change, or the multiple the market is willing to pay for them changes.

This is a way of looking, not a law

P/E is a definition, so Price = EPS × P/E is always true. What is a model is the idea that the multiple drifts around a middle value over decades while earnings do the real climbing. That has broadly held for large US companies over the past century, but multiples have stayed high or low for ten years or more, and nothing guarantees the future looks like the past.

[[TOGGLE runs]]

Here is what just happened. Over twenty days this year's earnings did not change, so all of the movement was the multiple. The multiple is the market's running summary of everything it expects. How fast earnings will grow, how risky they are, what interest rates are, and also plain mood. Over a few weeks almost none of that changes much, so most of the wiggle is noise from the order book.

Over thirty years the earnings grew more than sevenfold, and that growth carried the price with it. The swings in the multiple were still there, as the gaps between the lines show. But they stayed within the same range in percent, while the earnings kept multiplying.

This is the core reason long term investing can work. If you hold for decades, what you mostly collect is the growth in the business. If you hold for minutes, what you mostly collect is the noise, and the noise is someone else's gain or loss.
