[[CH time|Time is the main ingredient]] [[STEP grow|Growth on growth]]

Ronald Read did not just collect his companies' profits. He fed them back in. To see why that matters so much, start with $1,000 that grows 10% a year.

After one year you have $1,100. The second year, the 10% is figured on $1,100, not on the original $1,000, so you gain $110 and end at $1,210. The extra $10 is growth on last year's growth. Growth that builds on earlier growth like this is called compounding.

The picture shows ten years of it. In each bar, the pale part is the $1,000 you started with. The outlined part is the plain 10% of that $1,000, a steady $100 a year. The amber part is everything extra that came from growth on growth.

[[FIG compound|$1,000 growing at 10% a year for ten years, split into the original money, plain growth and growth on growth.]]

Look at how the amber part behaves. In year 1 it is zero. By year 10 it is $594, more than a third of all the growth so far. From year 15 on it is more than half. Carry the same picture to year 30 and it is $13,449 of the $16,449 gained, about four fifths. The longer the money sits, the more of the gain is growth on growth. That is why time matters more than almost anything else in this chapter.

[[STEP history|What stocks have actually returned]]

To use compounding you need a growth rate, and a made up 10% proves nothing. So here is the real record.

A professor at New York University, Aswath Damodaran, keeps a widely used table of US stock returns going back to 1928. It measures the S&P 500, an index of large US companies that [[C baskets]] explains, with all dividends reinvested. Price change plus dividends is called the total return, and every figure in this chapter is a total return.

From 1928 through 2025 the total return was about 10.0% a year, compounded. That is the compounded average, the one steady rate that turns $1 into $11,600 over 98 years. If you simply add up the 98 yearly returns and divide by 98, you get a higher number, about 11.9%, because a loss hurts more than an equal gain helps. Lose 50% and you need a 100% gain to get back. For growth over time, the compounded average is the one that matters.

But a 2025 dollar buys far less than a 1928 dollar. The general rise in prices over time is called inflation, and over this period it averaged about 3% a year. Taking inflation out is a division, not a subtraction. Each year a dollar grew to $1.100 while prices grew to $1.030, so buying power grew 1.100 ÷ 1.030 = 1.068 times, or about 6.8% a year. Subtracting, 10 − 3 = 7, is a quick estimate that is close when the rates are small. The after inflation figure is called the real return. The 10% is the nominal return, counted in dollars of the day.

History is not a promise

These are measurements of what happened in one country over one century, a century in which the US economy grew enormously. The S&P 500 itself began in 1957, and the earlier years use the S&P's smaller index of about 90 large companies. It is the best evidence there is, and it is still no guarantee of the next fifty years.

[[STEP smooth|Never smooth]]

A 10% average sounds calm. The years it is made of were not. Each thin bar below is one calendar year. Bars above the middle line were gains and bars below it were losses. The dashed line is the 10% compounded average.

[[FIG years|Each bar is one year of the S&P 500 with dividends reinvested, 1928 to 2025, from Damodaran's table. The 26 bars below the line are the losing years.]]

Count the bars below the line. In 26 of those 98 years stocks lost money, more than one year in four. In 1931 they fell about 44%. In 2008, the year Lehman Brothers failed, they fell about 37%. And calendar years hide the worst runs. The four years 1929 through 1932, one after another, turned $1 into about 35 cents.

The 10% average is built out of all of these bars. Investing Week 3 is about what years like those feel like and how people survive them.

[[STEP rule72|The rule of 72]]

There is a quick way to feel what a growth rate means. Divide 72 by the yearly percentage. The answer is roughly how many years it takes for money to double. This shortcut is called the rule of 72.

At 10% a year, money doubles in about 72 ÷ 10 = 7.2 years. The exact answer is 7.3 years. At 6.8% it doubles in about 72 ÷ 6.8 = 10.6 years, and the exact answer is 10.5. At 2% it takes about 36 years.

Now stack doublings. At 10%, forty years holds about 40 ÷ 7.3, five and a half doublings. 2⁵ is 32 and 2⁶ is 64, and five and a half doublings land between them, at 2⁵·⁵, about 45. That is why a dollar left alone for forty years at 10% becomes about $45.

Why 72?

The exact doubling time is ln 2 ÷ ln(1 + r). For small rates ln(1 + r) is close to r itself, so the doubling time is close to 0.693 ÷ r, which is 69.3 divided by the percentage. But ln(1 + r) is always a little smaller than r, and the gap grows with the rate, so near 8% the number that fits best is about 72. 72 also divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12. Between about 2% and 15% the shortcut is off by less than 4%.

[[STEP read|What it would have taken]]

Nobody published how much Ronald Read put in each year or what his exact returns were, so we cannot reconstruct his account. What we can do is check what his result would take.

Suppose someone invests $2,000 at the end of every year for 55 years, from 1959, the year of Read's earliest reported purchase, and earns 10% a year, roughly the long run average. In 1959 that was a lot of money for a mechanic. Read did spend about $2,380 on a single purchase that year, 39 shares of Pacific Gas and Electric. So either he saved a very large share of what he earned from early on, or his picks did better than the market's average, or both. The Journal called him a good stock picker. Nobody published the numbers that would settle it. What the check does show is that the engine is ordinary. Decades of saving plus growth on growth gets a modest earner into the millions.

Lehman Brothers was a Wall Street bank founded in 1850. Bankruptcy is the legal process for a company that cannot pay its debts. On September 15, 2008 Lehman filed for it, the largest bankruptcy in US history. The people it owed money to were paid what could be recovered, and the owners of its shares, who are paid last, were essentially wiped out. A share that had traded for more than $60 the year before became worth almost nothing.

Ronald Read owned Lehman shares. If they had been all he owned, his fortune would have vanished. Instead he barely noticed, because Lehman was one company out of dozens.

[[STEP spread|Your turn. One basket or ninety five]]

Flip between the two pictures below. In both, one company fails. The only thing that changes is how many companies the same money was spread across.

[[TOGGLE baskets]]

Here is what just happened. Owning many companies is called diversification. It works because a single company's disaster, a fraud, a lawsuit, a bad bet, a new competitor, mostly happens to that company alone. Spread across 95 companies, any one failure is a small dent, and the other 94 keep earning profits.

It also means you do not have to be right about every company. Read was wrong about Lehman and still ended with nearly $8 million.

[[STEP index|Owning the whole list]]

Diversification taken to its natural end means owning a little of everything. That idea needs two more words.

An index is a list of companies plus a rule for how much of each the list holds. The S&P 500 is the best known. It holds about 500 of the largest US companies, each weighted by its market cap, counting only shares that are available to trade, so a company whose market cap is twice as large counts twice as much. The S&P 500 number you see on the news is calculated from all their share prices.

You have met this before. The ES futures contract in the trading track is a bet on exactly this number. A trader in ES is betting on where the index goes next. An owner of the companies in it is collecting their profits.

A fund is a pool of money from many people that is invested as one, with each person owning a slice of the pool. A fund that simply buys every company in an index, in the index's proportions, is called an index fund. Investing Week 2 is entirely about them, including why most professional stock pickers fail to beat them over long periods.

The other reason to own everything

A study of every US stock from 1926 to 2016 found that the best 4% of companies produced all of the stock market's gain over Treasury bills, the safest short term loans to the US government. The other 96% together did no better than Treasury bills. You cannot know in advance which few will be the winners. Owning all of them is the only way to be sure you hold them.

[[STEP limits|What diversification cannot do]]

Owning many companies protects you from one company's bad luck. It does not protect you from all of them falling at once. In 1931 and in 2008 nearly every company fell together, because the whole economy was in trouble. A diversified owner fell too, by about the market's 44% and 37%.

So diversification shrinks the risk that comes from any one company. The standard theory in finance says investors are not paid extra for carrying that kind of risk, because anyone can get rid of it almost for free by owning more companies. What is left is the risk of the market as a whole. That is the risk long term owners are paid to sit through, and Investing Week 3 is about it.

Now go back to the zero sum idea from Trading Week 1. An ES futures contract is an agreement between two people about a price. Every ES contract has two sides. The trader who agreed to buy is said to be long, and the trader who agreed to sell is said to be short. When the price rises 10 points, the long side gains 10 points and the short side loses exactly 10, so the total for everyone is zero before fees.

A share is different in kind, not just in degree. It is a piece of a business, and the business makes new money every year by selling things people want. That profit did not come out of any other investor's pocket. It came from customers, and it belongs to the owners.

[[FIG games|Left, a futures contract, where gains and losses cancel. Right, customers' purchases become profit, which reaches all the owners by three paths.]]

Look at the bottom of each side. On the left the total is zero, every time. On the right the total is the company's profit, year after year, delivered as dividends, buybacks and a growing business. That is why owning companies is called positive sum.

[[STEP sort|Your turn. Profits or a transfer?]]

Below are six situations. For each one, decide whether money is being created by a business and flowing to its owners, or whether it is only moving from one person to another. The difference is the whole chapter, so take your time on the ones that involve stocks but are not ownership.

[[CLASSIFY games]]

The traps were the third and fifth. Trading a share is not the same as owning a business. Hold it for five minutes, or profit only from a change in the multiple, and you are playing the transfer game, even though the thing you hold is a share.

Keep these two apart

The profits are positive sum. The price changes that come from the multiple are not. If you buy at a P/E of 30 and sell at 15, you lose half your money even though the business earned the same. The investor who sold to you at 30 got a high price, and the one who buys from you at 15 gets the same earnings for half the price. Owners as a group collect the earnings. Individuals who buy high and sell low can still do badly, and Investing Week 7 is about why so many do.

[[STEP who|Who paid Ronald Read]]

Now the one question can be answered.

His money came from the profits of the companies he owned. Every time someone bought Tide, Smucker's jam, a prescription at CVS, or paid the interest on a loan from JPMorgan Chase, a little of the profit was his. The companies sent some of it to him as dividends, which he used to buy more shares. They spent some buying back shares from owners who wanted out, which handed the profit to him as a bigger slice of each company. And they reinvested the rest, which grew the profits that the next decades would deliver.

On top of that he had two things most people do not use. He had time, more than five decades by the Journal's account of a purchase he made in 1959, which let growth on growth do most of the work. And he rarely sold, so he paid very little in trading costs and never handed his gains to someone on the other side of a panic.

So who lost what he won? Nobody had to. That is the difference between owning a business and betting on its next price.

[[STEP why|Why one game pays owners by default and the other pays almost nobody]]

Put the two games side by side one last time. In day trading ES, the money you make has to come from another trader, and after fees the whole group loses. The Taiwan study found that fewer than 1 in 100 day traders could beat the market after fees, reliably, year after year.

In long term ownership, the profits of the businesses flow to all the owners by default. You do not need to beat anyone to receive your share of them. You need to own many businesses, ideally a whole index, keep your costs low, and stay owners through the bad years. Most people who do those three things for decades end up close to the market's return for their period, minus their costs, whatever that return turns out to be.

Beating other owners is still zero sum

Owning the market is positive sum. Trying to beat the market is not. Before costs, the stock pickers as a group hold, in effect, the market, so as a group they must earn the market's return. Every dollar one of them earns above it, another earns below it. After costs the group falls behind. Investing Week 2 builds this.

This is a description of how the two games are built, not advice to play one or the other. The trading track exists to find out honestly whether you can be in the 1%. This track exists so that whatever the answer, you understand the game where the odds are built in your favor.

Looking at any company you might own

  1. Remember what you would be buying. A fraction of a business that pays its owners last and can never take more than you paid. [[S promise]]
  2. Find the market cap and remember it is a price, not a value. [[S cap]]
  3. Find the EPS and how the company delivers it, as dividends, buybacks or reinvestment. [[S choices]]
  4. Compute the P/E and ask how much of the price is earnings and how much is the multiple. [[S pe]]
  5. Think in decades and real returns, and use the rule of 72 to feel what a rate means. [[S rule72]]
  6. Ask what share of the whole basket this one company would be. [[S spread]]

The whole story in one breath

A share is a fraction of a real business, and the business's profits belong to its owners, who are paid last and can never lose more than they put in, and who receive those profits as dividends, as buybacks that enlarge their slice, and as a business that grows. Day to day the price is pushed around by the order book and by changes in the multiple, but over decades it follows the earnings. Ronald Read owned dozens of ordinary companies for most of his life, reinvested what they paid him, and let more than five decades of growth on growth turn a mechanic's and janitor's savings into nearly $8 million, and because those profits came from customers rather than from other traders, nobody had to lose for him to win.
