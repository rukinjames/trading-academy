[[CH sides|Who is on the other side]] [[STEP aggressor|Equal amounts, unequal hurry]]

Go back to the second warm up question. Every contract bought is a contract sold, so buying and selling are always exactly equal. Saying there were more buyers than sellers is, taken literally, impossible.

What can be unequal is urgency. In every trade one side was waiting and one side arrived and crossed the spread. The one who crossed is called the aggressor. The aggressor is the same trader [[C orders]] called the taker. Taker describes what their order did to the book. Aggressor describes who started the trade. You will hear both words, and they point at the same person. On the tape in [[C price]] the colored dot showed exactly this, blue when the buyer crossed and red when the seller did. When aggressive buyers keep taking the asks faster than new asks arrive, the price climbs. When aggressive sellers keep hitting the bids, it falls.

So the useful question about any market move is never who is buying. It is who is in a hurry, and who is willing to wait for them.

[[STEP mm|The people who wait for a living]]

Someone has to be willing to wait on both sides, all day, or there would be no book to trade against. The firms that do this as a business are called market makers. A market maker posts a bid and an ask at the same time, say 5,999.75 and 6,000.00. If one trader sells to their bid and another buys from their ask, the market maker has bought low and sold high and earned the spread, one tick, without caring which way the market went.

That is the dream. The risk is that the two halves do not arrive together. Contracts a market maker holds while it waits for the other half are called inventory. Step through one market maker's bad afternoon. Its orders are labeled MM, and the line under the book shows its inventory.

[[STEPPER mm]]

Here is what just happened. Sellers kept hitting its bid while nobody bought from its ask, so it piled up contracts while the price fell. It is the adverse selection problem from [[C orders]] at industrial scale.

So a market maker has one strong defense. When the market becomes confusing or violent, it widens its prices, shrinks its sizes, or pulls its orders entirely, exactly as in stage 4. Its liquidity is a choice it makes second by second, not a promise.

Today most market making is done by computer programs that react in millionths of a second. Firms whose programs trade at that speed are called high frequency traders, or HFTs. Not every HFT is a market maker, but most market making is high frequency.

What was a stub quote?

In 2010 some market makers in stocks were required to keep a bid and an ask in the book at all times. When they did not want to trade, they met the rule with a bid of one cent and an ask of $100,000, prices so absurd nobody would ever hit them. Those placeholder orders were called stub quotes. Hold onto that, because on May 6 somebody hit them.

[[STEP others|Everyone else in the book]]

The other big groups in ES are easy to describe by what they need.

  • Institutions. Pension funds, mutual funds and insurers move huge amounts, often to protect a portfolio, which is called hedging. Their orders are far too big for the book, so they cut them into small slices and feed them in over time with computer programs called execution algorithms.
  • Short term professionals. Proprietary trading firms, which trade their own money, and hedge funds trying to profit from moves lasting seconds to days.
  • Retail traders. Individuals trading their own accounts. That is you, and everyone in TJR's Discord.
[[STEP zerosum|Every point you win, someone loses]]

Now one fact that should shape how you think about every trade you take. ES is a futures contract, and in futures every contract that exists has exactly one owner who gains when the price rises and one who gains when it falls. If the price goes up one point, every point gained by one side is a point lost by the other.

Before fees, all the profits and all the losses in ES add up to exactly zero. Fees go to the exchange and to brokers, so after fees the total is below zero. The spread is different. It never leaves the market. It moves from the takers who pay it to the makers who earn it. A market like this is called zero sum, and after fees negative sum.

So when you make money day trading ES, it is not created by the economy growing. It comes out of someone else's account. Your profit has to be paid by someone who is in more of a hurry than you, who knows less than you, or who needs to trade for reasons that have nothing to do with the next ten minutes, like a fund protecting a portfolio. That is a model, and it is a simplification, but it is a useful one. Every strategy you test in this course should come with an answer to one question. Who is paying me, and why?

Keep these two apart

Owning stock is not zero sum, because a share is a piece of a company, and the company's profits flow to its owners. An ES contract is not a piece of anything. It is an agreement between two people about a price, so whatever one side gains the other loses, whether they hold it for a minute or a year.

[[MCQ Q: A market maker is quoting 5,999.75 bid and 6,000.00 ask in ES. Suddenly prices are falling fast and nobody knows why. What is the market maker most likely to do, and why? - Buy more aggressively, because prices are cheap + Widen or pull its quotes, because sellers keep filling its bid and it is piling up inventory as the price drops - Keep quoting exactly as before, because it is required to F: A market maker earns the spread only when buys and sells arrive in balance. Whatever one group gains, the others lose. Company profits do not enter into it over a single day. ]] [[CH replay|Two thirty two to two forty five, replayed]] [[STEP algo|Read the seller's instructions again]]

You now know enough to read the official account of May 6 the way a trader would. It was published that September in a joint report by the two US regulators of these markets, the SEC for stocks and the CFTC for futures.

The report says the selling program was told to sell 75,000 ES contracts by targeting 9 percent of the previous minute's trading volume, and to do it without regard to price or time. Volume is simply the number of contracts that traded.

Translate that into the words from this page. Without regard to price means the program put no floor under what it would accept. Some of its sell orders rested in the book as asks, and some crossed the spread and hit the bids. The report counted both. Of the first 35,000 contracts it sold, about half were taker fills and about half were maker fills. Either way it kept selling however low the price went, and its resting asks kept pulling the best ask lower. Targeting a share of volume means its speed was tied to how much trading was happening around it. The more trading it saw, the faster it sold.

The report notes that the same firm had sold 75,000 contracts once before. That time it used a mix of human traders and programs that watched price and time as well as volume, and it took more than five hours. This time the program watched only volume, and it finished in about twenty minutes.

Before you read on, predict. What happens to a program like that if trading volume suddenly explodes?

[[STEP potato|The hot potato]]

At first the selling was absorbed. High frequency traders and other buyers took the contracts. The report says HFTs had bought about 3,300 more contracts than they had sold. But they are market makers at heart and do not want to hold inventory in a falling market, so between 2∶41 and 2∶44 they sold about 2,000 of those contracts back into the market as aggressors.

Then something strange happened. With no buyers stepping in who actually wanted to keep the contracts, the HFTs began passing contracts back and forth among themselves. Between 2∶45∶13 and 2∶45∶27, a span of fourteen seconds, HFTs traded over 27,000 contracts, about 49 percent of all volume, while their net buying was only about 200 contracts. The regulators called this hot potato trading. The same contracts were bought and sold over and over, and almost nobody was actually keeping them. The two bars below are drawn to scale.

[[FIG potato|Contracts HFTs traded in fourteen seconds, compared with the contracts they actually kept.]]

Look at the blue sliver. Nearly all of that volume was the same contracts changing hands.

Now apply your prediction. All through those minutes the selling program watched volume, and volume kept climbing. So it sold faster, into a market where almost nobody was really buying.

Volume is not liquidity

This was one of the regulators' main lessons. Volume counts how much traded. Liquidity is how much is waiting in the book, ready to absorb a big order without the price moving. On May 6 volume was enormous and liquidity was almost gone. It still happens. CME reported that on April 7, 2025, ES volume was about double its usual level while the size waiting at the front of the book was down by about two thirds.

[[STEP empty|The waiting room empties]]

The report measured the book itself. By 2∶45∶28 there were fewer than 1,050 contracts of resting buy orders left in ES, less than 1 percent of the buy side depth at the start of the day. The two bars below are drawn to scale.

[[FIG depth|Resting buy orders in ES at the start of the day, compared with 2∶45∶28 pm.]]

Look at how short the second bar is. Now remember the widget in [[S fire]]. A market order walks the book level by level, and the thinner the book, the further each order walks. ES fell about 3 percent from 2∶41 to 2∶44, then another 1.7 percent in just fifteen seconds, to a low of 1,056. Counting the seconds in between, the fall from 2∶41 was more than 5 percent. In 2010 the S&P 500 was far lower than today, so ES traded near 1,100 that afternoon, not near 6,000 like the made up books on this page.

[[STEP pause|Five seconds of quiet]]

Then CME's automatic safety system, called Stop Logic, switched on. Its job is to watch for a burst of triggered stop orders about to drive the price too far too fast, which is exactly the cascade from [[S stops]]. At 2∶45∶28 it froze trading in ES for five seconds. During the pause traders could still send and cancel orders, but nothing could match. In those five seconds the selling pressure partly eased and buy orders arrived. When trading resumed at 2∶45∶33, prices stabilized, and within minutes ES began to recover.

[[MCQ Q: Why did the 75,000 contract sale on May 6 do so much more damage than the firm's earlier sale of the same size, which took more than five hours? - Because 75,000 contracts was a huge share of that day's trading + It sold with no price limit at a speed tied to volume, and hot potato trading inflated volume while the book emptied, so it sold fast into almost no real buyers - Because high frequency traders refused to trade at all F: The size was the same. The fall in ES spread into stocks through one simple trade. When ES got cheap compared with the stocks it tracks, firms bought ES and at the same moment sold those stocks and the funds that hold them, to lock in the difference. That kind of trade is called arbitrage, and every one of those trades carried selling from ES into stocks. Then, around 2∶45, the report says, many automated liquidity providers in stocks paused, some because they feared their price data was wrong. Some widened their prices, some cut their sizes, and many withdrew entirely. Meanwhile stop loss sell orders were being triggered by the falling prices and met almost no buying interest.

Step through what that does to the book of a single stock. The sizes are made up. The stub quote at one cent is real.

[[STEPPER crash]]

Here is what just happened, and it is the whole answer.

A market sell order has one instruction, sell now to the best bid that exists. It does not check whether that bid is sane. When the real bids were pulled, the best bid that existed was a market maker's one cent placeholder, so that is where the trade happened. The one cent seller was not choosing one cent. They had chosen a market order, or a stop that becomes one, and the book chose one cent for them.

Now go back to the widget in [[S fire]] and pick the third preset, a stock at 2∶47 pm on May 6. Sell 1,000 shares and read your average price.

[[STEP answer|The answer to the one question]]

A price is the last trade between two orders. Behind it there is only the book, the list of people willing to wait, and that list exists only as long as each of them keeps choosing to stay. On May 6 a seller who did not care about price met a book whose depth had been drained. The firms that normally wait pulled out, stops turned into market orders, and market orders accepted the best bid that was left. For Accenture, for a moment, the best bid was one cent.

The aftermath fits the same picture. Between 2∶40 and 3∶00 more than 20,000 trades in over 300 securities, many of them funds that hold baskets of stocks, executed at prices 60 percent or more away from where they had been at 2∶40. After the close the exchanges cancelled those trades as clearly erroneous. Later that year regulators banned stub quotes and added automatic five minute pauses for single stocks that move 10 percent in five minutes.

Notice what the cancellation means for you. Nobody would have cancelled a trade that went 3 percent against you because the book was thin. They only cancelled the absurd ones. Ordinary slippage in a thin book is your cost, every time.

What the 2010 report did not know

Five years later, US authorities charged a London trader named Navinder Sarao with spoofing ES on May 6. Spoofing means placing large orders you intend to cancel, so that other traders think there is more buying or selling than there really is. That afternoon his program kept thousands of contracts of sell orders in the book that he never meant to trade. In 2016 he pleaded guilty, and the regulator's order says his orders added to the imbalance that day. How much of the crash he caused is still argued about. The mechanism on this page does not change. It adds one more reason the book looked worse for buyers than it really was, and one more reason to remember that the book is only promises.

Could this happen in ES today?

Not down to a penny. CME rejects orders priced too far from the market, gives every market and stop order a protection limit, pauses trading for a few seconds when the price moves too far too fast, and has limits that halt the market after large daily moves. Slippage in a thin book is still real. A protected order can still walk several points before it stops, and that cost is yours.

Every trading video you have watched is built around a chart, and almost always the chart is made of candles. Now that you know what a price is, you can see exactly what a candle is.

Pick a window of time, say one minute. Take every trade on the tape in that minute. From them, keep four numbers. The open is the price of the first trade in the window. The high is the highest price any trade reached. The low is the lowest. The close is the price of the last trade. Those four numbers, written OHLC, are the whole candle.

The thick part, called the body, spans from the open to the close. The thin lines above and below it, called wicks, reach to the high and the low. Here is how the two kinds look in this lesson.

[[FIG anatomy|An up candle, where the close is above the open, drawn hollow. A down candle, where the close is below the open, drawn filled.]]

Look at which end of the body is the open in each candle. On the hollow one the open is at the bottom, because the price rose. On the filled one the open is at the top. On TradingView and on TJR's charts the same candles are colored green and red. This page uses hollow and filled because red already means sellers here. Under most charts there is also a bar showing volume, the number of contracts that traded in that window.

Two things to take from that. The candle changes only when a trade sets a new high, sets a new low, or becomes the latest price. Everything else that happened is gone. And the candle can flip between filled and hollow during the minute, so a candle that is still forming can mislead you until it closes.

[[STEP nest|Candles nest inside each other]]

The length of a candle's window is called the timeframe. A 1 minute chart and a 5 minute chart show the same trades on two timeframes. A 5 minute candle is not a separate kind of thing. It is just a candle drawn over a longer window, so you can build it from five 1 minute candles. Its open is the open of the first one. Its close is the close of the last one. Its high is the highest high among the five, and its low is the lowest low.

[[FIG five|Five 1 minute candles of ES, numbered in time order, and the single 5 minute candle they make.]]

Check it against the picture, where dotted gold lines carry each value across. The open of candle 1 is 6,000.00 and the close of candle 5 is 6,001.00. The highest high is in candle 3 at 6,002.50, and the lowest low is in candle 4 at 5,998.75. Those four numbers are exactly the 5 minute candle on the right. This matters later, because TJR style analysis constantly switches between timeframes, and all of them are the same tape cut into different windows.

Here is what just happened. In minute A buyers won early, lost the middle, and won the end. A candle also hides everything you spent this week learning. It does not show the book. It does not show who was the aggressor. Either way, nothing in the candle tells you the cause.

That is not a reason to ignore charts. It is the reason this course will treat every chart pattern you learn in Weeks 4 to 7 as a claim to be tested on data, not a fact to be believed. When a video tells you what a candle shape means, remember that the shape is four numbers, and the story is a guess about a path nobody drew.

Before you send any order

  1. Read the best bid, the best ask and the spread. Remember the last price is just the most recent trade. [[S spread]]
  2. Decide whether you need to trade now or can wait. Now means a taker order and paying the spread. Waiting means a limit order and the risk of no fill or a bad fill. [[S bargain]]
  3. Compare your size with the depth at the best levels and estimate your average price before you press anything. [[S fire]]
  4. Ask where other people's stops are likely to sit, because those are hidden market orders. [[S stops]]
  5. Remember that thin books, the seconds around news, and fast markets make every taker order more expensive. [[S empty]]
  6. Read the chart as a summary of trades. It shows where price went, never the book, and never why. [[S hides]]

The whole story in one breath

A price is only the last trade between a buyer and a seller. Most of the time one of them was waiting in the order book with a limit order and the other arrived with a market order and crossed the spread. Prices move when takers eat through the waiting orders, and they move furthest when the book is thin. On May 6, 2010, a seller who ignored price met a book that market makers had emptied, stop orders turned into market orders, and a share of Accenture sold for one cent because one cent was the best bid left.
