Trading Academy. Week 1 of 12. Foundations

A share for one cent

On one afternoon in 2010 a share that had been trading at forty dollars sold for a penny, and nothing about the company had changed. By the end of this page you will be able to explain exactly how, and every piece of that explanation is something you will touch on every trade you ever make. You will be quizzed the whole way.

[[CH open|Forty dollars, then one cent]] [[STEP may6|The afternoon of May 6, 2010]]

It was a Thursday, and it was already a nervous day. Greece was close to defaulting on its debt, and US stocks had been sliding since the morning.

At 2∶32 in the afternoon, a large mutual fund company in Kansas, later named in the press as Waddell and Reed, switched on a computer program. (The firm has always disputed how much of what followed was its doing.) Its job was to sell 75,000 contracts of something called the E-mini S&P 500, worth about 4.1 billion dollars. The firm wanted protection in case the stock market kept falling.

The E-mini S&P 500, usually just called ES, is a futures contract whose price tracks the S&P 500, an index made from the stock prices of 500 large US companies. Week 2 explains exactly what a futures contract is. For now you only need one fact about it. It is one of the most heavily traded contracts in the world, and together with its Nasdaq cousin, NQ, it is what day traders like TJR mostly trade.

Between 2∶41 and 2∶45, in under five minutes, ES fell more than 5 percent. Then the damage spread into ordinary stocks. Accenture, a huge consulting company, was trading near $40 at 2∶46. At 2∶47 trades printed at one cent, meaning they were recorded on the official list of trades. By 2∶50 it was back at $39.51. Those minutes became known as the flash crash.

Here is the afternoon on one line, using only times and prices from the official report.

[[FIG timeline|May 6, 2010, from 2∶32 to 3∶08 pm, New York time. Open dots are events in ES, gold dots are trades in Accenture, and the gray dot is the five second pause.]]

Look at how short the gap is between the low in ES and the one cent trade in Accenture. Everything this page explains happened inside those two minutes. Other prints went absurdly the other way. Later that afternoon, at about 3∶29, two trades in Apple, 895 shares in all, printed at $100,000 a share, when Apple had been trading a little under $250 at 2∶30.

[[STEP question|The one question]]

Look at what changed for Accenture in that one minute. Its offices, its clients, its contracts and its profits were exactly the same at 2∶47 as they were at 2∶46. Nobody had discovered a scandal. The company did not change at all.

And yet a share of it changed hands for one cent.

So this whole page answers one question. What is a price, really, such that the same share can sell for forty dollars and for one cent within the same few minutes?

This matters to you more than it seems. Every trade you ever make goes through the same kind of machine, an order book, and that afternoon shows exactly what one does under stress. The way you send an order decides what you pay on every single trade, and over hundreds of trades that cost is often the difference between a strategy that makes money and one that loses it. By the last chapter you will replay the crash yourself and watch the one cent trade happen.

First, a quick check of where your picture of a price starts.

[[MCQ Q: Before reading on, which of these is closest to how a stock or futures price is set? - The exchange calculates a fair price from the company's value and posts it + It is simply the price at which the most recent trade happened - It is an average of what all buyers and sellers would like to pay F: The price on every screen is the most recent trade, nothing more. Start with the smallest possible market. One person wants to buy one contract and another wants to sell one. If they agree on a number, say 6,000.25, the contract changes hands at 6,000.25. That event is a trade, and 6,000.25 is the price of that trade.

ES prices are counted in index points, not dollars. What one point is worth in dollars, and why you never pay the whole price up front, is Week 2. For this week a point is just one whole step of the price, from 6,000 to 6,001. The prices on this page are round, made up numbers near 6,000. In November 2026 real ES was trading near 7,400.

Now scale it up to thousands of people trading thousands of times a second. Every trade gets recorded with its time, its price and its size, in a running list called the time and sales, or the tape. The price you see on any screen, the big number on a chart or a phone app, is just the price of the most recent trade on that list. It is called the last price, or simply the last.

Here is a short stretch of the tape. Each row is one trade. The dot shows which side arrived and traded at once instead of waiting, blue for a buyer and red for a seller. [[C sides]] gives that side its name.

[[FIG tape|Five trades from a made up minute of ES. Each row is one trade, with its time to the thousandth of a second, its price, and how many contracts changed hands.]]

Look at the bottom row. That trade, 6 contracts at 6,000.25, is the price, which is why only it is solid gold. One second earlier the price was 6,000.00, because the trade before it happened at 6,000.00. There is nothing behind these numbers. No hidden true value is stored anywhere in the system.

Keep these two apart

Value is what someone thinks a company or contract is worth. Price is a record of what two people actually agreed on a moment ago. They are usually close, because people trade near what they think things are worth. On May 6 they came apart, and nothing in the machinery forces them back together except people choosing to trade.

[[STEP meet|Buyers and sellers never meet]]

In real markets the buyer and the seller never see each other. They do not even arrive at the same moment. A buyer might decide to buy at 10∶41 and the seller who eventually fills the order might show up at 10∶43.

So the market needs a place where an order can wait until a counterpart arrives. A counterpart, or counterparty, is the person on the other side of your trade. An exchange is a company that runs such a marketplace and sets its rules. For ES the exchange is the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, owned by CME Group, and its computers sit in a data center in Aurora, Illinois, about 35 miles west of Chicago. The computer that does the work is called the matching engine, and the electronic market it runs is called Globex. It keeps a list of everyone who is waiting, and the instant two orders agree on a price, it matches them into a trade.

Your order does not go straight to Aurora. Here is the path every order takes, from top to bottom.

[[FIG orderpath|The path of an order. Each box is a separate company or system. The arrow on the right is the stream of trades and book updates sent back to every trader's screen.]]

Your app sends the order to your broker, the company that holds your account, which checks you have enough money and passes it on. The matching engine matches it. After a match, a third organization, CME Clearing, steps in between the two sides and guarantees the trade, so you never have to trust the stranger on the other side. Week 2 explains why that guarantee matters.

That list of everyone waiting is the most important object in this whole course. It has a name, and the next chapter draws it.

[[MCQ Q: At 2∶49 on May 6 a share of Accenture traded at one cent. Using only what you have read so far, what must have been true at that moment? + Someone agreed to sell at one cent, and someone had an order in place to buy at one cent - The exchange decided Accenture was worth one cent - Accenture's value had briefly dropped to one cent F: A price is a trade, and a trade needs two agreeing sides. Somebody was willing to sell for a penny and somebody had a standing order to buy for a penny. Who would ever do either is the mystery the rest of the page solves. ]] [[CH book|The waiting room]] [[STEP limit|A limit order is a promise with a price]]

The most common way to wait is a limit order. It says the quantity you want and the worst price you will accept. Buy 5 contracts at 5,999.75 or lower. Sell 3 contracts at 6,000.50 or higher. If nobody is on the other side at that price or better, the order sits in the matching engine and waits.

A waiting buy order is called a bid. A waiting sell order is called an ask, or an offer. Both words mean the same thing on the sell side.

The full list of all waiting limit orders, sorted by price, is the order book. The picture below is how traders draw it, and it is how every book in this course will look. Each row is one allowed price. In ES prices move in steps of 0.25 points, and one step is called a tick. Blue bars on the left are bids, and the number beside each bar is how many contracts are waiting there. Red bars on the right are asks.

[[FIG book|An order book. Rows are prices a quarter point apart. Blue bars are contracts waiting to buy, red bars contracts waiting to sell, and the outline marks the spread.]]

Read it from the middle outward. The highest blue bar is at 5,999.75. Nobody is offering to pay more than that right now, so 5,999.75 is the best bid. The lowest red bar is at 6,000.25. Nobody is willing to sell for less, so 6,000.25 is the best ask.

Trading platforms show this same picture, live, and call it the DOM, short for depth of market, or simply the ladder. It usually shows only the best ten or so prices on each side, so the screen is a partial view of the matching engine's full list. Above it, most platforms print a one line quote, for example Bid 5,999.75 × 5, Ask 6,000.25 × 4. The number after the × is the size waiting at that price.

How long does a limit order wait?

A limit order normally lasts until the end of the trading session unless you cancel it. That is called a day order. A good till cancelled order keeps waiting across sessions until it fills or you cancel it.

[[STEP spread|The gap that never closes]]

Between the best bid and the best ask there is always a gap. Here it is 6,000.25 minus 5,999.75, which is 0.50 points, or two ticks. That gap is the spread.

Why must there be a gap? Suppose a buyer posted a bid at 6,000.25, the same price as the best ask. A buyer willing to pay 6,000.25 and a seller willing to accept 6,000.25 agree, so the matching engine would trade them instantly. The bid would never get to sit in the book. So a resting bid is always below every resting ask. The moment they would touch, they trade.

The halfway point between best bid and best ask is called the mid price. Here it is 6,000.00. Notice that no trade has to happen at the mid. It is just arithmetic.

How wide is the spread in real ES?

ES trades almost around the clock, from Sunday evening to Friday afternoon New York time. The busiest stretch is the US stock market day, 9∶30 am to 4∶00 pm New York time. During that stretch the spread is almost always one tick, a quarter of a point, because so many traders compete to be at the front. The two tick spread in the picture is there so you can see the gap clearly.

[[STEP depth|Depth, the part of the book behind the front]]

The spread only describes the front of the book. Everything behind the best bid and the best ask, all the bars further out, is called depth. Depth tells you how much could trade before the price has to move to a new level.

The two books below have exactly the same best bid, best ask and spread. Flip between them and look at what sits behind the front. The bars in both are drawn to the same scale.

[[TOGGLE depth]]

Here is what just happened. A quote line that says Bid 5,999.75, Ask 6,000.00 is identical in both books, so the spread cannot warn you. Only the depth tells you that a modest order would barely touch one book and blow straight through the other. Keep that in mind, because on May 6 the depth is what disappeared.

Often many traders want the same price. Five people might all be bidding 5,999.75. When a seller arrives at that price, who gets the trade?

On CME Globex, for ES, the answer is whoever got there first. The matching engine keeps every price level as a line, in the order the orders arrived. This rule is called price time priority. The best price trades first, and among orders at the same price, the earliest one trades first. It is also called FIFO, short for first in, first out. The picture shows three sellers lined up at one price and a buyer arriving.

[[FIG queue|One price level drawn as a line of three sell orders. Gold is the part that traded.]]

Look at seller C. C wanted to sell at exactly the same price as A, and still sold nothing, purely because C arrived later. This is why speed matters so much to the firms that make markets. Being first in line is worth money, and some firms spend millions on equipment to save a few millionths of a second.

For you it means something simpler. If you join the best bid with a limit order, you go to the back of a line that may hold hundreds of contracts. The price can touch your level and leave again without you ever being filled. And if you change your order's price, it counts as a new arrival and goes to the back of the new line.

Not every market works this way

Some contracts on CME, mostly certain interest rate futures, split incoming trades among everyone at the price in proportion to their size instead. ES and the Nasdaq contract NQ, the ones this course uses, are first in, first out.

[[CH orders|The ways to send an order]] [[STEP market|The market order]]

A limit order says how much and at what worst price, and then it may wait. There is a second kind of order that never waits. A market order says only how much. Buy 10 now. It trades immediately against whatever is resting on the other side, starting at the best price and working outward until it is filled.

[[STEP makers|Makers and takers]]

Every trade now has two kinds of trader in it. The one whose order was resting in the book is called the maker, because their order made the market that the other person traded against. The one who arrived and traded at once is called the taker.

Resting orders are what let someone trade instantly, and that supply of waiting orders is what traders mean by liquidity. Makers provide liquidity. Takers use it up.

A market buy always trades at the ask, and a market sell always trades at the bid. So if you buy with a market order and immediately sell with another, you lose the spread, even if the price never moves. The spread is the price of being in a hurry.

[[CUSTOM fire]]

Three things to take from that.

First, a small market order pays exactly the best price. One contract bought at the best ask moves nothing except the size that was waiting there.

Second, a big market order eats through the book one level at a time. It takes everything at the best ask, then everything at the next price, and so on. Your average price ends up worse than the best price you saw when you pressed the button. That difference is the slippage. You did not just pay the price. You moved it, and the last trade ended up where your order stopped.

Third, the same order does far more damage in a thin book. Buy 10 costs nothing extra in the normal book and walks three levels in the thin one. Nothing about the contract changed between the first two presets. Only the depth did.

[[STEP whymoves|Your turn. Three ways a price moves]]

You have just seen the main way a price moves. But it is not the only one, and two of the pictures you have already seen showed the others. Step through the three ways below. Each one starts from the same book as the figure in [[S limit]].

[[STEPPER three]]

Here is what just happened.

Way 1 is the widget. Takers eat every order at the best level, so the next trade has to happen one level further out.

Way 2 needs no trade at all. The waiting orders themselves move. Researchers who measure prices over very short periods find that new and cancelled waiting orders matter as much as market orders do. This is how news usually reaches a price. Market makers pull their quotes and post new ones at a new level, often within a thousandth of a second, sometimes before anyone crosses the spread.

Way 3 is not movement. It is the tape from [[S trade]] again. Buyers trade at the ask and sellers at the bid, so the last price hops across the spread while nothing changes. It is called bid ask bounce, and on a 1 minute chart it can look like a fight that is not happening.

So when a trader says buyers are in control, the honest translation is that buy orders are taking the asks faster than sellers replace them, or sellers are moving their asks higher.

If market orders pay the spread, why not always use limit orders and collect it instead?

Because waiting has its own price. The first part is obvious. A limit order might never fill. You can bid 5,999.75, watch the market touch 5,999.75 while you sit at the back of the line, and then watch it rise two points without you.

The second part is subtle and it matters more. Think about when your resting buy order does get filled. It gets filled when somebody sells to you, and that most often happens when sellers are hitting the bids hard, which is exactly when the price is about to go lower. Your limit buy tends to fill on the moves that go against you, and tends to miss the moves that go your way. This trap is called adverse selection. The orders that fill you are, on average, from the people who are in a hurry, and sometimes they are in a hurry because they know something.

So neither order is free. A market order pays a known, small cost every time. A limit order pays an unknown cost in missed trades and in bad fills. Choosing between them is a real decision on every trade, and you will practice it.

In one breath

A limit order rests and waits, provides liquidity, and risks never filling or filling at the worst moment. A market order takes liquidity, fills now, and pays the spread plus slippage. The trade always happens at the waiting order's price.

[[STEP stops|The order nobody can see]]

There is one more order type, and it is the one that turns a dip into a collapse. A stop order is an instruction that sits outside the book, completely invisible to other traders. It contains a trigger price. Nothing happens until a trade prints at the trigger price or beyond it. Then the stop wakes up and becomes a market order. On CME that market order carries a built in limit called protection, a few points past the trigger, so it can walk down the book but not forever.

The most common use is the stop loss. You buy at 6,000.00 and place a stop sell with a trigger at 5,997.00. If the price falls to 5,997.00, your stop becomes a market sell and gets you out before the loss grows. Almost every disciplined trader uses one, and you will too.

Now picture many traders who all bought near the same price and all put their stops just below the same obvious level. Step through what happens when the price reaches it. The dashed box is the group of stop orders, drawn outside the book because the book does not contain them.

[[STEPPER stops]]

Here is what just happened. One ordinary sale of 6 contracts touched the trigger, and that one trade released 20 contracts of market selling that had been hidden until that instant. The selling ate four levels of bids. A small push became a fast drop, made entirely of other people's protective orders.

This is a big idea and it will come back. In Week 5 you will study why prices so often seem to be pulled toward obvious highs and lows. One explanation, which you will test on data instead of believing, is this picture. Clusters of stops are clusters of future market orders. Nobody can see them, but anyone who needs to buy or sell a lot can guess where they probably sit.

How traders actually place stops

Most platforms let you attach a stop and a profit target to your entry as one package, called a bracket. The stop and the target wait together. When one of them fills, the platform cancels the other. That pairing is called OCO, one cancels other. Almost every TJR style trade is entered this way.

You might be wondering

Is there a version that cannot slip like that? Yes. A stop limit order becomes a limit order instead of a market order when triggered, so it will never fill at a terrible price. The cost is that in a fast drop it may not fill at all, and you stay in a losing trade while the price falls past you.
