Chapter 1 Forty dollars, then one cent

Step 1. The afternoon of May 6, 2010

It was a Thursday, and it was already a nervous day. Greece was close to defaulting on its debt, and US stocks had been sliding since the morning.

At 2∶32 in the afternoon, a large mutual fund company in Kansas, later named in the press as Waddell and Reed, switched on a computer program. (The firm has always disputed how much of what followed was its doing.) Its job was to sell 75,000 contracts of something called the E-mini S&P 500, worth about 4.1 billion dollars. The firm wanted protection in case the stock market kept falling.

The E-mini S&P 500, usually just called ES, is a futures contract whose price tracks the S&P 500, an index made from the stock prices of 500 large US companies. The unit on futures contracts (Unit 3) explains exactly what one is. For now you only need one fact about it. It is one of the most heavily traded contracts in the world, and together with its Nasdaq cousin, NQ, it is what day traders like TJR mostly trade.

Between 2∶41 and 2∶45, in under five minutes, ES fell more than 5 percent. Then the damage spread into ordinary stocks. Accenture, a huge consulting company, was trading near $40 at 2∶46. At 2∶47 trades printed at one cent, meaning they were recorded on the official list of trades. By 2∶50 it was back at $39.51. Those minutes became known as the flash crash.

Here is the afternoon on one line, using only times and prices from the official report.

Look at how short the gap is between the low in ES and the one cent trade in Accenture. Everything this page explains happened inside those two minutes. Other prints went absurdly the other way. Later that afternoon, at about 3∶29, two trades in Apple, 895 shares in all, printed at $100,000 a share, when Apple had been trading a little under $250 at 2∶30.

Step 2. The one question

Look at what changed for Accenture in that one minute. Its offices, its clients, its contracts and its profits were exactly the same at 2∶47 as they were at 2∶46. Nobody had discovered a scandal. The company did not change at all.

And yet a share of it changed hands for one cent.

So this whole page answers one question. What is a price, really, such that the same share can sell for forty dollars and for one cent within the same few minutes?

This matters to you more than it seems. Every trade you ever make goes through the same kind of machine, an order book, and that afternoon shows exactly what one does under stress. The way you send an order decides what you pay on every single trade, and over hundreds of trades that cost is often the difference between a strategy that makes money and one that loses it. By the last chapter you will replay the crash yourself and watch the one cent trade happen.

First, a quick check of where your picture of a price starts.

Quick quiz

Before reading on, which of these is closest to how a stock or futures price is set?

The price on every screen is the most recent trade, nothing more. Exchanges do publish a few official prices, like the daily settlement you will meet in Unit 3, but those are computed from trades too, never from what a company is worth. The next chapter shows why that one fact makes a one cent trade possible.

When more people want to buy a stock than sell it, what happens to the number of shares bought compared with the number sold that day?

Every share bought was sold by someone. Each trade has exactly one buyer and one seller for the same quantity. What can be unbalanced is not the amount bought and sold but how eager each side is. Chapter 5 is about exactly that.

Chapter 2 A price is the last handshake

Step 3. A trade is two people agreeing

Start with the smallest possible market. One person wants to buy one contract and another wants to sell one. If they agree on a number, say 6,000.25, the contract changes hands at 6,000.25. That event is a trade, and 6,000.25 is the price of that trade.

ES prices are counted in index points, not dollars. What one point is worth in dollars, and why you never pay the whole price up front, is the unit on futures contracts (Unit 3). For now a point is just one whole step of the price, from 6,000 to 6,001. The prices on this page are round, made up numbers near 6,000. In November 2026 real ES was trading near 7,400.

Now scale it up to thousands of people trading thousands of times a second. Every trade gets recorded with its time, its price and its size, in a running list called the time and sales, or the tape. The price you see on any screen, the big number on a chart or a phone app, is just the price of the most recent trade on that list. It is called the last price, or simply the last.

Here is a short stretch of the tape. Each row is one trade. The dot shows which side arrived and traded at once instead of waiting, blue for a buyer and red for a seller. Chapter 5 gives that side its name.

Look at the bottom row. That trade, 6 contracts at 6,000.25, is the price, which is why only it is solid gold. One second earlier the price was 6,000.00, because the trade before it happened at 6,000.00. There is nothing behind these numbers. No hidden true value is stored anywhere in the system.

Keep these two apart

Value is what someone thinks a company or contract is worth. Price is a record of what two people actually agreed on a moment ago. They are usually close, because people trade near what they think things are worth. On May 6 they came apart, and nothing in the machinery forces them back together except people choosing to trade.

Step 4. Buyers and sellers never meet

In real markets the buyer and the seller never see each other. They do not even arrive at the same moment. A buyer might decide to buy at 10∶41 and the seller who eventually fills the order might show up at 10∶43.

So the market needs a place where an order can wait until a counterpart arrives. A counterpart, or counterparty, is the person on the other side of your trade. An exchange is a company that runs such a marketplace and sets its rules. For ES the exchange is the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, owned by CME Group, and its computers sit in a data center in Aurora, Illinois, about 35 miles west of Chicago. The computer that does the work is called the matching engine, and the electronic market it runs is called Globex. It keeps a list of everyone who is waiting, and the instant two orders agree on a price, it matches them into a trade.

Your order does not go straight to Aurora. Here is the path every order takes, from top to bottom.

Your app sends the order to your broker, the company that holds your account, which checks you have enough money and passes it on. The matching engine matches it. After a match, a third organization, CME Clearing, steps in between the two sides and guarantees the trade, so you never have to trust the stranger on the other side. Unit 3 explains why that guarantee matters.

That list of everyone waiting is the most important object in this whole course. It has a name, and the next chapter draws it.

Quick quiz

At 2∶47 on May 6 a share of Accenture traded at one cent. Using only what you have read so far, what must have been true at that moment?

A price is a trade, and a trade needs two agreeing sides. Somebody was willing to sell for a penny and somebody had a standing order to buy for a penny. Who would ever do either is the mystery the rest of the page solves.

Chapter 3 The waiting room

Step 5. A limit order is a promise with a price

The most common way to wait is a limit order. It says the quantity you want and the worst price you will accept. Buy 5 contracts at 5,999.75 or lower. Sell 3 contracts at 6,000.50 or higher. If nobody is on the other side at that price or better, the order sits in the matching engine and waits.

A waiting buy order is called a bid. A waiting sell order is called an ask, or an offer. Both words mean the same thing on the sell side.

The full list of all waiting limit orders, sorted by price, is the order book. The picture below is how traders draw it, and it is how every book in this course will look. Each row is one allowed price. In ES prices move in steps of 0.25 points, and one step is called a tick. Blue bars on the left are bids, and the number beside each bar is how many contracts are waiting there. Red bars on the right are asks.

Read it from the middle outward. The highest blue bar is at 5,999.75. Nobody is offering to pay more than that right now, so 5,999.75 is the best bid. The lowest red bar is at 6,000.25. Nobody is willing to sell for less, so 6,000.25 is the best ask.

Trading platforms show this same picture, live, and call it the DOM, short for depth of market, or simply the ladder. It usually shows only the best ten or so prices on each side, so the screen is a partial view of the matching engine's full list. Above it, most platforms print a one line quote, for example Bid 5,999.75 × 5, Ask 6,000.25 × 4. The number after the × is the size waiting at that price.

How long does a limit order wait?

A limit order normally lasts until the end of the trading session unless you cancel it. That is called a day order. A good till cancelled order keeps waiting across sessions until it fills or you cancel it.

Step 6. The gap that never closes

Between the best bid and the best ask there is always a gap. Here it is 6,000.25 minus 5,999.75, which is 0.50 points, or two ticks. That gap is the spread.

Why must there be a gap? Suppose a buyer posted a bid at 6,000.25, the same price as the best ask. A buyer willing to pay 6,000.25 and a seller willing to accept 6,000.25 agree, so the matching engine would trade them instantly. The bid would never get to sit in the book. So a resting bid is always below every resting ask. The moment they would touch, they trade.

The halfway point between best bid and best ask is called the mid price. Here it is 6,000.00. Notice that no trade has to happen at the mid. It is just arithmetic.

How wide is the spread in real ES?

ES trades almost around the clock, from Sunday evening to Friday afternoon New York time. The busiest stretch is the US stock market day, 9∶30 am to 4∶00 pm New York time. During that stretch the spread is almost always one tick, a quarter of a point, because so many traders compete to be at the front. The two tick spread in the picture is there so you can see the gap clearly.

Step 7. Depth, the part of the book behind the front

The spread only describes the front of the book. Everything behind the best bid and the best ask, all the bars further out, is called depth. Depth tells you how much could trade before the price has to move to a new level.

The two books below have exactly the same best bid, best ask and spread. Flip between them and look at what sits behind the front. The bars in both are drawn to the same scale.

Hundreds of contracts wait at every price. A market buy for 50 is swallowed by the first level and the price does not move at all.

Exactly the same best bid, best ask and spread. But only a handful of contracts wait at each price, and some prices are empty. The bars are drawn to the same scale as the deep book, which is why they are barely visible. A market buy for 50 uses up every ask on screen after 11 contracts.

Here is what just happened. A quote line that says Bid 5,999.75, Ask 6,000.00 is identical in both books, so the spread cannot warn you. Only the depth tells you that a modest order would barely touch one book and blow straight through the other. Keep that in mind, because on May 6 the depth is what disappeared.

Step 8. Your turn. Build a book one order at a time

Step through the six stages below. Each stage adds one event, except stage 4, which fast forwards through several new orders. Read the line under each picture before you press Next, and watch for the moment something turns gold, because gold always means a trade actually happened.

Here is what just happened.

For four stages nothing traded at all. Orders arrived and waited, and the book grew. A book can be full of activity and still have no trades.

Then in stage 5 a seller arrived whose price reached the best bid. The trade happened at 5,999.75, the price of the order that was already waiting. That is a general rule. When a new order matches a resting one, the trade happens at the resting order's price. The trader who waited set the price, and the trader who arrived accepted it.

And in stage 6 someone simply left. Nobody is obliged to keep an order in the book.

Quick quiz

A book shows best bid 6,000.00 and best ask 6,000.50. What are the spread and the mid price?

The spread is 6,000.50 minus 6,000.00, which is 0.50 points, two ticks. The mid is halfway between them, 6,000.25. No trade needs to happen at the mid. It is just the center of the gap.

The best ask is 6,000.25. A buyer sends a limit buy for 2 contracts at 6,000.50. At what price do they trade?

The buyer was willing to pay up to 6,000.50, but a seller was already waiting at 6,000.25. Trades happen at the resting order's price, so the buyer gets the better price, 6,000.25. A limit is the worst price you accept, not the price you must pay.

Why can a resting bid never be at or above the best ask?

A bid at or above the best ask agrees with a seller on price, so the matching engine trades them on the spot. Whatever remains in the book afterwards has to sit below the asks. What people want has nothing to do with it. It is the matching rule.

Step 9. First come, first served

Often many traders want the same price. Five people might all be bidding 5,999.75. When a seller arrives at that price, who gets the trade?

On CME Globex, for ES, the answer is whoever got there first. The matching engine keeps every price level as a line, in the order the orders arrived. This rule is called price time priority. The best price trades first, and among orders at the same price, the earliest one trades first. It is also called FIFO, short for first in, first out. The picture shows three sellers lined up at one price and a buyer arriving.

Look at seller C. C wanted to sell at exactly the same price as A, and still sold nothing, purely because C arrived later. This is why speed matters so much to the firms that make markets. Being first in line is worth money, and some firms spend millions on equipment to save a few millionths of a second.

For you it means something simpler. If you join the best bid with a limit order, you go to the back of a line that may hold hundreds of contracts. The price can touch your level and leave again without you ever being filled. And if you change your order's price, it counts as a new arrival and goes to the back of the new line.

Not every market works this way

Some contracts on CME, mostly certain interest rate futures, split incoming trades among everyone at the price in proportion to their size instead. ES and the Nasdaq contract NQ, the ones this course uses, are first in, first out.

Quick quiz

A screen shows ES with a last price of 6,012.25. What does that number tell you?

The last price is a record of the most recent trade. It is not a measure of worth, and it is not an offer. If you buy right now you pay the best ask, which may be higher.

The best bid is 5,999.75 with 40 contracts waiting, and the best ask is 6,000.25. A seller sends a limit sell for 3 contracts at 5,999.50. What happens?

A limit is the worst price you accept. A buyer is already waiting at 5,999.75, which is better for the seller than 5,999.50, so the matching engine trades them at once, at the waiting order's price.

Two ES books have the same best bid, the same best ask and the same spread. What can still be very different between them?

The quote line only shows the front of the book. Depth, everything waiting behind the best bid and best ask, decides whether a modest order barely touches the book or walks straight through it. The tick size is fixed by the contract, and the last price does not decide where the next trade happens.

Chapter 4 The ways to send an order

Step 10. The market order

A limit order says how much and at what worst price, and then it may wait. There is a second kind of order that never waits. A market order says only how much. Buy 10 now. It trades immediately against whatever is resting on the other side, starting at the best price and working outward until it is filled.

Step 11. Makers and takers

Every trade now has two kinds of trader in it. The one whose order was resting in the book is called the maker, because their order made the market that the other person traded against. The one who arrived and traded at once is called the taker.

Resting orders are what let someone trade instantly, and that supply of waiting orders is what traders mean by liquidity. Makers provide liquidity. Takers use it up.

A market buy always trades at the ask, and a market sell always trades at the bid. So if you buy with a market order and immediately sell with another, you lose the spread, even if the price never moves. The spread is the price of being in a hurry.

Three things to take from that.

First, a small market order pays exactly the best price. One contract bought at the best ask moves nothing except the size that was waiting there.

Second, a big market order eats through the book one level at a time. It takes everything at the best ask, then everything at the next price, and so on. Your average price ends up worse than the best price you saw when you pressed the button. That difference is the slippage. You did not just pay the price. You moved it, and the last trade ended up where your order stopped.

Third, the same order does far more damage in a thin book. Buy 10 costs nothing extra in the normal book and walks three levels in the thin one. Nothing about the contract changed between the first two presets. Only the depth did.

Step 13. Your turn. Three ways a price moves

You have just seen the main way a price moves. But it is not the only one, and two of the pictures you have already seen showed the others. Step through the three ways below. Each one starts from the same book as the figure in Step 5.

Here is what just happened.

Way 1 is the widget. Takers eat every order at the best level, so the next trade has to happen one level further out.

Way 2 needs no trade at all. The waiting orders themselves move. Researchers who measure prices over very short periods find that new and cancelled waiting orders matter as much as market orders do. This is how news usually reaches a price. Market makers pull their quotes and post new ones at a new level, often within a thousandth of a second, sometimes before anyone crosses the spread.

Way 3 is not movement. It is the tape from Step 3 again. Buyers trade at the ask and sellers at the bid, so the last price hops across the spread while nothing changes. It is called bid ask bounce, and on a 1 minute chart it can look like a fight that is not happening.

So when a trader says buyers are in control, the honest translation is that buy orders are taking the asks faster than sellers replace them, or sellers are moving their asks higher.

Quick quiz

The asks are 10 contracts at 6,000.25, 10 at 6,000.50 and 20 at 6,000.75. You send a market buy for 25. What is your average price?

You take 10 at 6,000.25, 10 at 6,000.50, and the last 5 at 6,000.75. That costs 60,002.50 plus 60,005.00 plus 30,003.75, which is 150,011.25 for 25 contracts. Divide and you get 6,000.45. The best ask was 6,000.25, so your slippage is 0.20 points per contract.

In the problem above, what is the last price right after your order?

Your final 5 contracts traded at 6,000.75, so that is the most recent trade, and the most recent trade is the price. One order moved the price two ticks.

You buy 1 ES contract with a market order when the book is best bid 5,999.75 and best ask 6,000.00, then instantly sell it with another market order. Nothing else changes. What happened?

You bought at the ask, 6,000.00, and sold at the bid, 5,999.75. The market did not move at all and you are still down one tick. Every round trip with market orders starts with this loss, before any fees.

Step 14. What a limit order costs you instead

If market orders pay the spread, why not always use limit orders and collect it instead?

Because waiting has its own price. The first part is obvious. A limit order might never fill. You can bid 5,999.75, watch the market touch 5,999.75 while you sit at the back of the line, and then watch it rise two points without you.

The second part is subtle and it matters more. Think about when your resting buy order does get filled. It gets filled when somebody sells to you, and that most often happens when sellers are hitting the bids hard, which is exactly when the price is about to go lower. Your limit buy tends to fill on the moves that go against you, and tends to miss the moves that go your way. This trap is called adverse selection. The orders that fill you are, on average, from the people who are in a hurry, and sometimes they are in a hurry because they know something.

So neither order is free. A market order pays a known, small cost every time. A limit order pays an unknown cost in missed trades and in bad fills. Choosing between them is a real decision on every trade, and you will practice it.

In one breath

A limit order rests and waits, provides liquidity, and risks never filling or filling at the worst moment. A market order takes liquidity, fills now, and pays the spread plus slippage. The trade always happens at the waiting order's price.

Step 15. The order nobody can see

There is one more order type, and it is the one that turns a dip into a collapse. A stop order is an instruction that sits outside the book, completely invisible to other traders. It contains a trigger price. Nothing happens until a trade prints at the trigger price or beyond it. Then the stop wakes up and becomes a market order. On CME that market order carries a built in limit called protection, a few points past the trigger, so it can walk down the book but not forever.

The most common use is the stop loss. You buy at 6,000.00 and place a stop sell with a trigger at 5,997.00. If the price falls to 5,997.00, your stop becomes a market sell and gets you out before the loss grows. Almost every disciplined trader uses one, and you will too.

Now picture many traders who all bought near the same price and all put their stops just below the same obvious level. Step through what happens when the price reaches it. The dashed box is the group of stop orders, drawn outside the book because the book does not contain them.

Here is what just happened. One ordinary sale of 6 contracts touched the trigger, and that one trade released 20 contracts of market selling that had been hidden until that instant. The selling ate four levels of bids. A small push became a fast drop, made entirely of other people's protective orders.

This is a big idea and it will come back. In Unit 8, Liquidity, you will study why prices so often seem to be pulled toward obvious highs and lows. One explanation, which you will test on data instead of believing, is this picture. Clusters of stops are clusters of future market orders. Nobody can see them, but anyone who needs to buy or sell a lot can guess where they probably sit.

How traders actually place stops

Most platforms let you attach a stop and a profit target to your entry as one package, called a bracket. The stop and the target wait together. When one of them fills, the platform cancels the other. That pairing is called OCO, one cancels other. Almost every TJR style trade is entered this way.

You might be wondering

Is there a version that cannot slip like that? Yes. A stop limit order becomes a limit order instead of a market order when triggered, so it will never fill at a terrible price. The cost is that in a fast drop it may not fill at all, and you stay in a losing trade while the price falls past you.

Step 16. Your turn. Maker or taker?

Below are seven orders. For each one, decide whether it rests in the book and adds liquidity, or trades at once and takes liquidity. The book is the same in every case unless the order says otherwise. Best bid 5,999.75, best ask 6,000.00. Watch out for the ones where the type of order and what it actually does disagree.

Order 1. The book shows best bid 5,999.75 and best ask 6,000.00. You send a limit buy for 2 at 5,999.50.

Nobody is selling at 5,999.50 or lower. The cheapest seller wants 6,000.00. Your order cannot match, so it waits in the book below the best bid. You added liquidity.

Order 2. Same book. You send a market sell for 3.

A market order never waits. It sells to the best bid, 5,999.75, at once. You took liquidity and paid the spread to be immediate.

Order 3. Same book. You send a limit buy for 2 at 6,000.25.

Your limit says you will pay up to 6,000.25. A seller is already waiting at 6,000.00, which is better than your limit, so you match immediately and fill at 6,000.00. A limit order priced through the other side is called a marketable limit order, and it takes liquidity just like a market order.

Order 4. Same book. You send a limit sell for 1 at 6,000.50.

No buyer will pay 6,000.50. The highest bid is 5,999.75. Your order rests above the best ask and waits for a buyer to come up to it.

Order 5. Same book. You send a limit sell for 4 at 5,999.75.

A buyer is waiting at exactly 5,999.75. Your sell matches it at once. Selling at the bid means you are the one crossing the spread.

Order 6. You placed a stop sell with a trigger at 5,999.00 an hour ago. The price just traded at 5,999.00.

A stop order sits outside the book doing nothing until its trigger price trades. Then it becomes a market order and sells immediately to whatever bids are left.

Order 7. Same book. You send a limit buy for 1 at 5,999.75, the same price as the best bid.

You join the line of buyers at 5,999.75. Nobody is selling there, so you wait, and you wait behind everyone who was already in that line.

The traps in that set were orders three and five. A limit order is not automatically a maker. If its price reaches the other side of the book, it trades at once just like a market order. What decides whether you make or take is not the name of the order. It is whether your price reaches the waiting orders on the other side.

Quick quiz

You buy 1 ES contract with a market order and immediately sell it with another market order. The price never moved. What happened to your account, before fees?

A market buy trades at the ask and a market sell trades at the bid. The round trip costs the gap between them even if the price never moves, and fees come on top. The spread is the price of being in a hurry.

Why does a resting limit buy tend to get filled on the moves that go against you?

This is adverse selection. Your buy fills when somebody sells to you, and that happens most when sellers are in a hurry, which is exactly when the price tends to keep falling. The moves that go your way often leave without you.

You are long with a stop sell triggered at 5,995.00. Nothing has traded at 5,995.00 yet. Then a trade prints at 5,994.75. What happens to your stop?

A stop wakes up when a trade prints at the trigger price or beyond it, so 5,994.75 counts. It then becomes a market order and sells into whatever bids are left, on CME within its protection limit. A stop limit is the order that would rest as a limit instead.

Chapter 5 Who is on the other side

Step 17. Equal amounts, unequal hurry

Go back to the second warm up question. Every contract bought is a contract sold, so buying and selling are always exactly equal. Saying there were more buyers than sellers is, taken literally, impossible.

What can be unequal is urgency. In every trade one side was waiting and one side arrived and crossed the spread. The one who crossed is called the aggressor. The aggressor is the same trader Chapter 4 called the taker. Taker describes what their order did to the book. Aggressor describes who started the trade. You will hear both words, and they point at the same person. On the tape in Chapter 2 the colored dot showed exactly this, blue when the buyer crossed and red when the seller did. When aggressive buyers keep taking the asks faster than new asks arrive, the price climbs. When aggressive sellers keep hitting the bids, it falls.

So the useful question about any market move is never who is buying. It is who is in a hurry, and who is willing to wait for them.

Step 18. The people who wait for a living

Someone has to be willing to wait on both sides, all day, or there would be no book to trade against. The firms that do this as a business are called market makers. A market maker posts a bid and an ask at the same time, say 5,999.75 and 6,000.00. If one trader sells to their bid and another buys from their ask, the market maker has bought low and sold high and earned the spread, one tick, without caring which way the market went.

That is the dream. The risk is that the two halves do not arrive together. Contracts a market maker holds while it waits for the other half are called inventory. Step through one market maker's bad afternoon. Its orders are labeled MM, and the line under the book shows its inventory.

Here is what just happened. Sellers kept hitting its bid while nobody bought from its ask, so it piled up contracts while the price fell. It is the adverse selection problem from Chapter 4 at industrial scale.

So a market maker has one strong defense. When the market becomes confusing or violent, it widens its prices, shrinks its sizes, or pulls its orders entirely, exactly as in stage 4. Its liquidity is a choice it makes second by second, not a promise.

Today most market making is done by computer programs that react in millionths of a second. Firms whose programs trade at that speed are called high frequency traders, or HFTs. Not every HFT is a market maker, but most market making is high frequency.

What was a stub quote?

In 2010 some market makers in stocks were required to keep a bid and an ask in the book at all times. When they did not want to trade, they met the rule with a bid of one cent and an ask of $100,000, prices so absurd nobody would ever hit them. Those placeholder orders were called stub quotes. Hold onto that, because on May 6 somebody hit them.

Step 19. Everyone else in the book

The other big groups in ES are easy to describe by what they need.

  • Institutions. Pension funds, mutual funds and insurers move huge amounts, often to protect a portfolio, which is called hedging. Their orders are far too big for the book, so they cut them into small slices and feed them in over time with computer programs called execution algorithms.
  • Short term professionals. Proprietary trading firms, which trade their own money, and hedge funds trying to profit from moves lasting seconds to days.
  • Retail traders. Individuals trading their own accounts. That is you, and everyone in TJR's Discord.

Step 20. Every point you win, someone loses

Now one fact that should shape how you think about every trade you take. ES is a futures contract, and in futures every contract that exists has exactly one owner who gains when the price rises and one who gains when it falls. If the price goes up one point, every point gained by one side is a point lost by the other.

Before fees, all the profits and all the losses in ES add up to exactly zero. Fees go to the exchange and to brokers, so after fees the total is below zero. The spread is different. It never leaves the market. It moves from the takers who pay it to the makers who earn it. A market like this is called zero sum, and after fees negative sum.

So when you make money day trading ES, it is not created by the economy growing. It comes out of someone else's account. Your profit has to be paid by someone who is in more of a hurry than you, who knows less than you, or who needs to trade for reasons that have nothing to do with the next ten minutes, like a fund protecting a portfolio. That is a model, and it is a simplification, but it is a useful one. Every strategy you test in this course should come with an answer to one question. Who is paying me, and why?

Keep these two apart

Owning stock is not zero sum, because a share is a piece of a company, and the company's profits flow to its owners. An ES contract is not a piece of anything. It is an agreement between two people about a price, so whatever one side gains the other loses, whether they hold it for a minute or a year.

Quick quiz

A market maker is quoting 5,999.75 bid and 6,000.00 ask in ES. Suddenly prices are falling fast and nobody knows why. What is the market maker most likely to do, and why?

A market maker earns the spread only when buys and sells arrive in balance. In a one sided fall, its bid keeps getting filled and its inventory loses value every second. Pulling quotes is how it stops the bleeding. In ES no rule forces it to stay.

In ES, before any fees, if all day traders together made 10 million dollars of profit in a day, what must be true?

Every ES contract has a winning side and a losing side for each move, so the totals cancel exactly before costs. Whatever one group gains, the others lose. Company profits do not enter into it over a single day.

Chapter 6 Two thirty two to two forty five, replayed

Step 21. Read the seller's instructions again

You now know enough to read the official account of May 6 the way a trader would. It was published that September in a joint report by the two US regulators of these markets, the SEC for stocks and the CFTC for futures.

The report says the selling program was told to sell 75,000 ES contracts by targeting 9 percent of the previous minute's trading volume, and to do it without regard to price or time. Volume is simply the number of contracts that traded.

Translate that into the words from this page. Without regard to price means the program put no floor under what it would accept. Some of its sell orders rested in the book as asks, and some crossed the spread and hit the bids. The report counted both. Of the first 35,000 contracts it sold, about half were taker fills and about half were maker fills. Either way it kept selling however low the price went, and its resting asks kept pulling the best ask lower. Targeting a share of volume means its speed was tied to how much trading was happening around it. The more trading it saw, the faster it sold.

The report notes that the same firm had sold 75,000 contracts once before. That time it used a mix of human traders and programs that watched price and time as well as volume, and it took more than five hours. This time the program watched only volume, and it finished in about twenty minutes.

Before you read on, predict. What happens to a program like that if trading volume suddenly explodes?

Step 22. The hot potato

At first the selling was absorbed. High frequency traders and other buyers took the contracts. The report says HFTs had bought about 3,300 more contracts than they had sold. But they are market makers at heart and do not want to hold inventory in a falling market, so between 2∶41 and 2∶44 they sold about 2,000 of those contracts back into the market as aggressors.

Then something strange happened. With no buyers stepping in who actually wanted to keep the contracts, the HFTs began passing contracts back and forth among themselves. Between 2∶45∶13 and 2∶45∶27, a span of fourteen seconds, HFTs traded over 27,000 contracts, about 49 percent of all volume, while their net buying was only about 200 contracts. The regulators called this hot potato trading. The same contracts were bought and sold over and over, and almost nobody was actually keeping them. The two bars below are drawn to scale.

Look at the blue sliver. Nearly all of that volume was the same contracts changing hands.

Now apply your prediction. All through those minutes the selling program watched volume, and volume kept climbing. So it sold faster, into a market where almost nobody was really buying.

Volume is not liquidity

This was one of the regulators' main lessons. Volume counts how much traded. Liquidity is how much is waiting in the book, ready to absorb a big order without the price moving. On May 6 volume was enormous and liquidity was almost gone. It still happens. CME reported that on April 7, 2025, ES volume was about double its usual level while the size waiting at the front of the book was down by about two thirds.

Step 23. The waiting room empties

The report measured the book itself. By 2∶45∶28 there were fewer than 1,050 contracts of resting buy orders left in ES, less than 1 percent of the buy side depth at the start of the day. The two bars below are drawn to scale.

Look at how short the second bar is. Now remember the widget in Step 12. A market order walks the book level by level, and the thinner the book, the further each order walks. ES fell about 3 percent from 2∶41 to 2∶44, then another 1.7 percent in just fifteen seconds, to a low of 1,056. Counting the seconds in between, the fall from 2∶41 was more than 5 percent. In 2010 the S&P 500 was far lower than today, so ES traded near 1,100 that afternoon, not near 6,000 like the made up books on this page.

Step 24. Five seconds of quiet

Then CME's automatic safety system, called Stop Logic, switched on. Its job is to watch for a burst of triggered stop orders about to drive the price too far too fast, which is exactly the cascade from Step 15. At 2∶45∶28 it froze trading in ES for five seconds. During the pause traders could still send and cancel orders, but nothing could match. In those five seconds the selling pressure partly eased and buy orders arrived. When trading resumed at 2∶45∶33, prices stabilized, and within minutes ES began to recover.

Quick quiz

Why did the 75,000 contract sale on May 6 do so much more damage than the firm's earlier sale of the same size, which took more than five hours?

The size was the same. The difference was the instructions. The earlier sale also paid attention to price and time, and this one only watched volume, which came from HFTs trading with each other rather than from real buyers. And 75,000 was only about 1.3 percent of the 5.7 million ES contracts traded that day, so size alone does not explain it.

Five seconds of paused trading stopped the fall in ES. Using what you know about the book, what most plausibly happened in those five seconds?

Pausing stops market orders from hitting an empty book. It gives buyers who think the price is too low time to post bids. When trading resumed the book had depth again, so sells no longer walked straight down it.

Step 25. How a share reached one cent

The fall in ES spread into stocks through one simple trade. When ES got cheap compared with the stocks it tracks, firms bought ES and at the same moment sold those stocks and the funds that hold them, to lock in the difference. That kind of trade is called arbitrage, and every one of those trades carried selling from ES into stocks. Then, around 2∶45, the report says, many automated liquidity providers in stocks paused, some because they feared their price data was wrong. Some widened their prices, some cut their sizes, and many withdrew entirely. Meanwhile stop loss sell orders were being triggered by the falling prices and met almost no buying interest.

Step through what that does to the book of a single stock. The sizes are made up. The stub quote at one cent is real.

Here is what just happened, and it is the whole answer.

A market sell order has one instruction, sell now to the best bid that exists. It does not check whether that bid is sane. When the real bids were pulled, the best bid that existed was a market maker's one cent placeholder, so that is where the trade happened. The one cent seller was not choosing one cent. They had chosen a market order, or a stop that becomes one, and the book chose one cent for them.

Now go back to the widget in Step 12 and pick the third preset, a stock at 2∶47 pm on May 6. Sell 1,000 shares and read your average price.

Step 26. The answer to the one question

A price is the last trade between two orders. Behind it there is only the book, the list of people willing to wait, and that list exists only as long as each of them keeps choosing to stay. On May 6 a seller who did not care about price met a book whose depth had been drained. The firms that normally wait pulled out, stops turned into market orders, and market orders accepted the best bid that was left. For Accenture, for a moment, the best bid was one cent.

The aftermath fits the same picture. Between 2∶40 and 3∶00 more than 20,000 trades in over 300 securities, many of them funds that hold baskets of stocks, executed at prices 60 percent or more away from where they had been at 2∶40. After the close the exchanges cancelled those trades as clearly erroneous. Later that year regulators banned stub quotes and added automatic five minute pauses for single stocks that move 10 percent in five minutes.

Notice what the cancellation means for you. Nobody would have cancelled a trade that went 3 percent against you because the book was thin. They only cancelled the absurd ones. Ordinary slippage in a thin book is your cost, every time.

What the 2010 report did not know

Five years later, US authorities charged a London trader named Navinder Sarao with spoofing ES on May 6. Spoofing means placing large orders you intend to cancel, so that other traders think there is more buying or selling than there really is. That afternoon his program kept thousands of contracts of sell orders in the book that he never meant to trade. In 2016 he pleaded guilty, and the regulator's order says his orders added to the imbalance that day. How much of the crash he caused is still argued about. The mechanism on this page does not change. It adds one more reason the book looked worse for buyers than it really was, and one more reason to remember that the book is only promises.

Could this happen in ES today?

Not down to a penny. CME rejects orders priced too far from the market, gives every market and stop order a protection limit, pauses trading for a few seconds when the price moves too far too fast, and has limits that halt the market after large daily moves. Slippage in a thin book is still real. A protected order can still walk several points before it stops, and that cost is yours.

Quick quiz

A friend says the flash crash proves that Accenture's real value dropped for a few minutes. What is the best correction?

The trades really happened, which is why they had to be cancelled afterwards. But they reflected an empty book, not an opinion about the company. Price and value came apart because the machinery of waiting orders broke down.

Which order type protected sellers from being filled at one cent, and what did it risk?

A limit sell at, say, $39.00 can never fill below $39.00. On May 6 it would not have traded at one cent. The price of that protection is that if the price falls through your limit and keeps going, you are still holding the shares. A market order never fills at the last price on the screen. It fills at the best bid that exists, which is the whole lesson.

Sitting 4 of 4, about 35 minutes

Three questions on sitting 3 before you go on. Your answers and your place are saved, so a divider is a good place to stop or to start.

Quick quiz

A trade prints at the best bid. Who was the aggressor?

The bid is where buyers wait. A trade there means a seller crossed the spread and hit a waiting bid, so the seller started the trade. Aggressor and taker point at the same trader.

In ES, where does the spread that takers pay end up?

Fees leave the market and go to the exchange and brokers. The spread moves from the takers who pay it to the makers who earn it. That is why ES is zero sum before fees and negative sum after.

On April 7, 2025, ES volume was about double its usual level. Why was that no comfort to a trader who needed to sell a lot at once?

Volume counts how much traded. Liquidity is how much is waiting in the book to absorb the next order. On May 6 and again on April 7, 2025, volume was huge while the waiting size shrank, and a big market order walks further in a book like that however busy the tape looks.

Chapter 7 What your chart is made of

Step 27. A chart is a summary of the tape

Every trading video you have watched is built around a chart, and almost always the chart is made of candles. Now that you know what a price is, you can see exactly what a candle is.

Pick a window of time, say one minute. Take every trade on the tape in that minute. From them, keep four numbers. The open is the price of the first trade in the window. The high is the highest price any trade reached. The low is the lowest. The close is the price of the last trade. Those four numbers, written OHLC, are the whole candle.

The thick part, called the body, spans from the open to the close. The thin lines above and below it, called wicks, reach to the high and the low. Here is how the two kinds look in this lesson.

Look at which end of the body is the open in each candle. On the hollow one the open is at the bottom, because the price rose. On the filled one the open is at the top. On TradingView and on TJR's charts the same candles are colored green and red. This page uses hollow and filled because red already means sellers here. Under most charts there is also a bar showing volume, the number of contracts that traded in that window.

Step 28. Your turn. Watch a minute become a candle

On the left of each frame is every trade in one made up minute of ES, plotted in time order, each gold dot one trade. A real ES minute during the day often holds hundreds of trades. This one has thirteen so you can follow each one. On the right is the candle those trades have built so far. Step through and watch which events change the candle and which do not.

Two things to take from that. The candle changes only when a trade sets a new high, sets a new low, or becomes the latest price. Everything else that happened is gone. And the candle can flip between filled and hollow during the minute, so a candle that is still forming can mislead you until it closes.

Step 29. Candles nest inside each other

The length of a candle's window is called the timeframe. A 1 minute chart and a 5 minute chart show the same trades on two timeframes. A 5 minute candle is not a separate kind of thing. It is just a candle drawn over a longer window, so you can build it from five 1 minute candles. Its open is the open of the first one. Its close is the close of the last one. Its high is the highest high among the five, and its low is the lowest low.

Check it against the picture, where dotted gold lines carry each value across. The open of candle 1 is 6,000.00 and the close of candle 5 is 6,001.00. The highest high is in candle 3 at 6,002.50, and the lowest low is in candle 4 at 5,998.75. Those four numbers are exactly the 5 minute candle on the right. This matters later, because TJR style analysis constantly switches between timeframes, and all of them are the same tape cut into different windows.

Quick quiz

Three 1 minute candles in order are O 6,010.00 H 6,012.25 L 6,009.50 C 6,011.75, then O 6,011.75 H 6,011.75 L 6,007.00 C 6,008.25, then O 6,008.25 H 6,010.50 L 6,008.00 C 6,010.00. What is the 3 minute candle?

Open of the first, 6,010.00. Highest high, 6,012.25, in the first candle. Lowest low, 6,007.00, in the second. Close of the last, 6,010.00. The tempting wrong answer took the low from the last candle instead of the lowest of all three.

What does that 3 minute candle look like?

The open and the close are both 6,010.00, so the body has no height and the candle is a flat line with two wicks. Traders call a candle like this a doji, and you will hear the word in every TJR video. The fall inside the middle minute has vanished. Only the four numbers survive.

Step 30. Your turn. What a candle cannot tell you

Here are two different minutes of trading. Flip between them and compare the paths on the left, then compare the candles on the right.

Buyers push the price to the high in the first quarter of the minute. Then sellers drive it all the way to the low before buyers lift it to the close.

Sellers hit the low within ten seconds. Then buyers grind the price up to the high and it eases back to the close. A completely different fight, and the candle on the right has not changed at all.

Here is what just happened. In minute A buyers won early, lost the middle, and won the end. In minute B sellers won the first ten seconds, buyers won the middle, and sellers took a little back at the end. Those are opposite stories, and they produce exactly the same candle, because both have the same open, high, low and close.

A candle also hides everything you spent this unit learning. It does not show the book. It does not show who was the aggressor. It does not show the stops waiting below, or whether the volume was real buyers or HFTs passing a hot potato. A one minute candle drawn from that afternoon's raw trades in Accenture would show a wick down to one cent. Charts built after the trades were cancelled may not show it at all. Either way, nothing in the candle tells you the cause.

That is not a reason to ignore charts. It is the reason this course will treat every chart pattern you learn in the chart units, from Unit 6 to Unit 11, as a claim to be tested on data, not a fact to be believed. When a video tells you what a candle shape means, remember that the shape is four numbers, and the story is a guess about a path nobody drew.

Quick quiz

Part 1. What are the spread and the mid price?

Best ask 6,000.50 minus best bid 6,000.25 is 0.25, a single tick, which is normal for ES. The mid is halfway, 6,000.375, a price that cannot even trade, since ES moves in quarters.

Part 2. A trader sends a market buy for 15 contracts. What is their average price, to two decimals?

They take all 8 at 6,000.50, then 7 of the 12 at 6,000.75. That is 48,004.00 plus 42,005.25, which is 90,009.25, divided by 15, which is about 6,000.617. Slippage is about 0.12 points per contract compared with the best ask.

Part 3. Go back to the original book. Traders who bought earlier have stop sells totalling 20 contracts with a trigger at 6,000.25. A seller sends a market sell for 6. Where is the last price once everything has finished trading?

The 6 contract sell takes all 6 bids at 6,000.25. That trade touches the trigger, so 20 contracts of market sells wake up. They take all 15 at 6,000.00 and then 5 of the 25 at 5,999.75. One small sale moved the price two ticks, because it released orders nobody could see.

Part 4. You want to buy 2 contracts, you are in no hurry, and you think the price is fair right here. Which order fits, and what is the catch?

Joining the bid saves you the spread, but you go to the back of the line at 6,000.25. A limit buy at 6,001.00 would not rest at all, it would trade at once against the asks, starting at 6,000.50. Its price reaches the other side, so it is a taker.

Step 32. Mixed questions, the way the exam asks them

The unit exam mixes everything, so this last set does too. Answer each one before reading the feedback.

Quick quiz

Which of these would most likely make the ES spread widen?

Market makers fear being run over by a sudden move, so they step back right before scheduled news. With fewer orders at the front, the best bid and best ask move apart. A big resting bid makes the book deeper, not the spread wider.

The tape shows a big trade at the ask. Who was the aggressor?

A trade at the ask means a buyer accepted a resting sell order's price. The seller was waiting. The buyer arrived and crossed, so the buyer was the aggressor.

Why does a limit buy order tend to get filled right before the price falls further?

Your resting buy is filled by somebody else's urgency to sell. That urgency is itself a sign of falling prices. This is adverse selection, and it is the hidden cost of always waiting. The exchange plays no favorites. It just follows price time priority.

A candle's open is 6,004.00 and its close is 6,002.50. Its high is 6,006.25. Which statement must be true?

The close is below the open, so it is filled. The high is above the open, so there is an upper wick. You cannot tell whether the high came before the low or after it. That order is exactly what a candle throws away.

Before you send any order

  1. Read the best bid, the best ask and the spread. Remember the last price is just the most recent trade. Step 6
  2. Decide whether you need to trade now or can wait. Now means a taker order and paying the spread. Waiting means a limit order and the risk of no fill or a bad fill. Step 14
  3. Compare your size with the depth at the best levels and estimate your average price before you press anything. Step 12
  4. Ask where other people's stops are likely to sit, because those are hidden market orders. Step 15
  5. Remember that thin books, the seconds around news, and fast markets make every taker order more expensive. Step 23
  6. Read the chart as a summary of trades. It shows where price went, never the book, and never why. Step 30

The whole story in one breath

A price is only the last trade between a buyer and a seller. Most of the time one of them was waiting in the order book with a limit order and the other arrived with a market order and crossed the spread. Prices move when takers eat through the waiting orders, and they move furthest when the book is thin. On May 6, 2010, a seller who ignored price met a book that market makers had emptied, stop orders turned into market orders, and a share of Accenture sold for one cent because one cent was the best bid left.
