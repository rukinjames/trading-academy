Chapter 1 A box of paper in Brattleboro

Step 1. The will

Ronald Read was born in 1921 in Dummerston, a small town in southern Vermont. He walked or hitchhiked about four miles to get to high school, and in 1940 he became the first person in his family to graduate. He served in World War II and came home at the end of 1945.

Then he worked. For about 25 years he pumped gas and fixed cars at Haviland's service station in Brattleboro. He and his older brother later bought the station, and they sold it when he retired in 1979. Retirement lasted about a year. He then spent 17 years as a part time janitor at the local J. C. Penney store, until 1997.

He was famously careful with money. People remembered a fraying jacket he kept wearing by holding it together with a safety pin, a secondhand car, and a habit of parking far away to avoid paying the meter. He chopped his own firewood and picked up fallen branches for his wood stove. Few people had any hint. The clues were in what he read, The Wall Street Journal, Barron's, which is a weekly investing magazine, and books from the public library.

He died in June 2014, at 92. In February 2015 his will was made public, and the town found out. He left $4.8 million to Brattleboro Memorial Hospital and $1.2 million to Brooks Memorial Library, the largest gifts either had ever received. His whole estate came to nearly $8 million.

Here is his life on one line. Every date comes from news reports of his will and his obituary.

Step 2. What was in the box

A few weeks later The Wall Street Journal looked at what he had owned. Almost all of the money was in shares of companies, small pieces of ownership that the next chapter builds from scratch. Owning shares of a company is also called owning its stock. When he died he owned stock in at least 95 different companies, many of them held for decades. Among them were Procter and Gamble, which makes Tide and Crest, the bank JPMorgan Chase, General Electric, Johnson and Johnson, Dow Chemical, the jam maker J. M. Smucker, and the drugstore chain CVS.

He kept the paper certificates that proved his ownership in a safe deposit box at his bank. Stacked up, they were about five inches thick. He avoided technology companies and anything fashionable. And when his companies paid him cash, he generally used it to buy more of their shares.

He was not perfect at it. One of his companies was Lehman Brothers, the bank that collapsed in 2008. Its shares became essentially worthless. You will see in Chapter 6 why that barely mattered to him.

Step 3. The one question

Put this next to what researchers have found about day trading. One of the largest studies used every trade on the Taiwan Stock Exchange from 1992 to 2006. Fewer than 1 in 100 day traders could beat the market after fees, reliably, from one year to the next. And the trading track showed that in futures like ES, every point one trader wins, another loses.

Ronald Read never traded fast, never had special information, and never earned a large salary. Yet he ended up with nearly $8 million.

So this whole page answers one question. Where did his money come from, and who lost what he won?

The second half of that question has a surprising answer, and Chapter 7 gives it. First, a quick check of where your picture starts.

Why one man proves nothing

You hear about Ronald Read because his story is rare and surprising. Nobody writes about the frugal janitor who saved for fifty years and ended with an ordinary amount. So this page does not treat him as proof of anything. It uses him as a puzzle, and then checks his result against arithmetic and against a century of market data.

Quick quiz

Before reading on, what do you think owning a share of a company actually gives you?

A share is a piece of ownership. It is not a loan, and the company never has to pay it back. What you get is a fraction of the company and of what it earns. The next chapter builds that from scratch, including exactly what the share promises you.

Ronald Read held the same companies for decades and rarely sold. Which explanation fits that best?

Profits are new money the companies earn from their customers every year, and the owners are entitled to them. That is different from winning a trade against someone. The stock market does not only go up, and you will see its worst years in Chapter 5.

Chapter 2 What a share actually is

Step 4. A business owned by partners

Start with something small. Picture a late night ice cream shop owned by a few partners. The shop has freezers, a lease, a brand, and customers. Each year, after paying for ingredients, staff, rent and taxes, it has money left over. That leftover money is the profit, also called earnings.

The partners need a clear way to say who owns how much. So they cut the ownership into equal pieces, say 1,000 of them. Each piece is called a share. The total number of shares that exist is called the shares outstanding. If you own 50 of the 1,000 shares, you own 50 ÷ 1,000 = 5% of the shop. Loosely, that is five percent of the freezers, the brand, and every dollar of profit. Step 8 makes that sentence precise.

The picture below shows the same thing. Each block is 50 shares. Purple is your slice. The pale blocks belong to everyone else.

Notice what a share is not. It is not a bet on the next price, and it is not a loan. It is a fraction of a real business, and it keeps that meaning whether or not anyone ever trades it.

Step 5. The same idea, very large

A public company is one whose shares anyone can buy and sell on a stock exchange. It works exactly like the shop, just bigger. Procter and Gamble is cut into billions of shares instead of 1,000. The company does not sell them to you directly. Owners who want to sell meet buyers who want to buy in the same kind of order book you studied in Trading Week 1, and the price of a share is just the last trade.

One difference from ES. ES has a single book in Aurora. A US stock trades at the same time on more than a dozen exchanges, each with its own book of the same kind, and a large share of trades happen off the exchanges altogether. Most orders sent from phone apps are filled by big trading firms called wholesalers. The price you see is the last trade reported from any of these places.

The paper certificates in Ronald Read's box were the old way to prove ownership. Today almost nobody holds paper. Your broker keeps an electronic record that you own the shares, and the meaning is identical.

Step 6. What the market charges for the whole company

If you know the price of one share and the number of shares outstanding, you can work out what the market is charging for the entire company. Multiply them. The result is called the market capitalization, usually shortened to market cap.

Say a company has 2 billion shares outstanding and the last trade was $50. Its market cap is 2 billion × $50 = $100 billion.

Be careful with the word. Market cap is a price, not a value. Trading Week 1 showed that a price is only the last agreement between two orders. Market cap just multiplies that last agreement by the number of shares. If the price falls 10% in a nervous afternoon, the market cap falls 10% too, even though the factories and customers are exactly the same.

Step 7. Your slice of the profit

The most useful single number for an owner is the profit that belongs to one share. Take the company's earnings for a year and divide by the shares outstanding. That is earnings per share, written EPS.

A company that earns $6 billion with 2 billion shares outstanding has EPS of $6 billion ÷ 2 billion = $3. If you own 100 shares, $300 of that year's profit belongs to your shares, whether the company hands it to you or keeps it in the business. The next chapter is about which of those happens.

Step 8. What the share actually promises you

A share does not let you walk into the shop and take five percent of the freezers. Legally the company owns the freezers, and you own a piece of the company. That piece gives you three rights.

First, you get what is left over. Customers pay the company. Out of that money it pays suppliers, staff, rent, interest to anyone who lent it money, and taxes. Whatever remains is the profit, and only the owners have a claim on it. That is why owners are called residual claimants. Residual means left over.

Flip between the two years below. Only the sales change. Read each row from the top, and watch the bottom row, which is what reaches the owners.

A good year. Customers pay $100. Suppliers and staff, lenders and taxes are paid first, $80 in all. The last $20 is the profit, and it belongs to the owners.

A bad year. Only the sales change, to $70. Suppliers and staff still take $70, and the lenders are still owed their $5, so the company pays them from savings or new borrowing. There is no profit, so there is no tax and nothing for the owners. Owners are paid last, so the worst they get in a year is nothing.

Here is what just happened. In the good year the owners got the last $20. In the bad year everyone ahead of them was still owed the same, so nothing was left and the owners got nothing for that year. The lenders were still paid, out of the company's savings or new borrowing.

Second, you get a vote. Owners elect a board of directors, usually one vote per share. The board hires the managers and decides how much of the profit to pay out and how much to keep. You cannot demand a dividend. You can only vote for the people who decide.

Third, you can never lose more than you paid. If the company collapses owing billions, its lenders cannot come after you for the rest. That protection is called limited liability. It is a real difference from ES, where a bad enough move can cost you more than the money in your account. Trading Week 2 shows how.

Quick quiz

A company has 500 million shares outstanding and the last trade was $40. What is its market cap?

Market cap is shares outstanding times price. 500 million × $40 = $20,000 million, which is $20 billion. The $80 billion answer used 2 billion shares, the share count from the earlier example.

The same company earned $1.5 billion this year. What is its EPS?

EPS is earnings divided by shares outstanding. $1.5 billion ÷ 500 million = $3.00. Each share's slice of this year's profit is three dollars. The $0.30 answer dropped a zero, and $0.33 divided the wrong way round.

You own 2,000 of a company's 400,000 shares outstanding. What fraction of the company do you own?

2,000 ÷ 400,000 = 0.005, which is 0.5%. That means half a percent of the company and of what is left for its owners.

A company's share price falls 15% in one day on a rumor that turns out to be false. What happened to its market cap?

Market cap moves exactly with the price, since the number of shares did not change. That is why it measures what the market charges at the moment, not what the business is worth.

A company's sales fall and, after paying its suppliers, staff, lenders and taxes, nothing is left this year. What do its owners receive for the year?

Owners are residual claimants. They are paid last, out of whatever is left. When nothing is left they get nothing that year, and limited liability means they never get a bill for the company's losses.

You paid $2,000 for shares in a company that later collapses owing $5 billion more than it has. What is the most you can lose?

Limited liability caps an owner's loss at what was paid for the shares. The lenders take the loss on the $5 billion, and they cannot come after the owners for it.

Chapter 3 Where the money comes from

Step 9. Three things a company can do with its profit

Every year a profitable company has the same decision to make. Its profit belongs to its owners, but the board decides how to deliver it. There are three main ways.

It can pay some out in cash. It can use some to buy back its own shares. And it can keep the rest, to reinvest in the business by building new factories, hiring, or buying other companies, or to pay down what it owes. Here is one made up year.

Each path puts value in the owners' hands in a different way. The next three steps take them one at a time.

Step 10. Dividends, the cash path

Cash paid out to owners is called a dividend. It is set as an amount per share and usually paid every three months. If a company pays $1 per share each quarter and you own 100 shares, you receive $100 four times a year.

To compare dividends across companies with very different share prices, people divide the yearly dividend by the price. That is the dividend yield. A share at $50 that pays $2 a year has a yield of $2 ÷ $50 = 4%.

Some companies have paid for a very long time. Procter and Gamble, one of Ronald Read's companies, has paid a dividend every year since it became a corporation in 1890, and in April 2026 it raised the amount for the 70th year in a row.

Keep these two apart

A dividend is not free extra money on top of the share. Each dividend has a cutoff day, called the ex dividend date. Anyone who buys the share on or after that day does not get the coming payment. On that day the share price typically drops by about the dividend amount, because the cash is about to leave the company. The dividend is your profit being delivered, not a gift.

Step 11. Your turn. Buybacks, the invisible path

The second path is quieter. A company can use its cash to buy its own shares from owners who want to sell, and then cancel them. That is a buyback. Step through what it does to an owner who does nothing. Watch the purple block, the cash box on the right, and the line at the bottom.

Here is what just happened. The owners who sold received cash, just like a dividend, but only the ones who wanted it. Everyone who stayed now owns a bigger fraction of the same profit. Earnings per share rose from $10 to $12.50 even though the company earned exactly the same amount.

Now look at what it cost. The cash that paid the sellers was the company's cash, and a tenth of it was yours. You now own a bigger slice of a company that has less cash in it. If the company paid a fair price, those two changes cancel exactly, the same way a share price drops by the dividend on the ex dividend date. So a buyback is not new money. It is a second way of handing over profit the company already earned. It helps the owners who stay only when the company buys its shares for less than they are really worth, and it hurts them when it overpays.

This matters when you read company reports. A rising EPS can mean the business is growing, or it can mean the same profit is being divided among fewer shares. Only the first one makes the owners richer.

Step 12. Reinvestment, the slow path

The third path is the one you cannot see at all in the short run. Profit the company keeps is used to build the business. A drugstore chain opens new stores. A consumer goods company builds a new factory. If those projects earn more than the owners could have earned by putting the same money somewhere else, the company's future profits are larger, and so is each owner's slice of them.

This is where most of the long run growth in earnings comes from. It is also where things can go wrong. A company that wastes its kept profit on projects that earn too little makes its owners poorer. Judging that is part of what Investing Week 4 is about.

Notice what all three paths have in common. The money comes from customers buying the company's products, and it flows to the owners. Nobody on the other side of a trade had to lose it.

Quick quiz

A share trades at $80 and the company pays $0.80 per share every quarter. What is the dividend yield?

The yearly dividend is 4 × $0.80 = $3.20. Dividing by the price gives $3.20 ÷ $80 = 0.04, which is 4%. The 1% answer forgot that the $0.80 is paid four times a year.

A company earns $200 million and has 100 million shares. It buys back and cancels 20 million shares, and earnings stay the same. What is the new EPS?

Before, EPS was $200 million ÷ 100 million = $2.00. After the buyback 80 million shares remain, so EPS is $200 million ÷ 80 million = $2.50. Same profit, fewer slices.

Why does a share's price usually drop on its ex dividend date, the first day a buyer no longer gets the coming dividend?

That cash is about to leave the company for the owners' accounts. Each share now represents a company that will have less cash, and a buyer no longer gets the payment, so the price adjusts by roughly the dividend. An owner's total, share plus the coming cash, is unchanged at that moment.

A company uses $500 million of its cash to buy back shares at exactly what they are worth. Right after, are the owners who did not sell any richer?

Each remaining share does own more of the profit, but the $500 million that paid the sellers belonged partly to every owner. At a fair price those two changes cancel. The buyback hands over profit already earned, like a dividend, and leaves total profit unchanged.

A company keeps $100 million of profit and builds a factory that earns 2% a year. Its owners could have earned 7% a year by putting that money elsewhere. What did the reinvestment do for them?

The test for reinvestment is not whether a project earns something. It is whether it earns more than the owners could have got elsewhere. Earning 2% when 7% was available costs the owners about 5% a year on that $100 million.

Chapter 4 Why the price bounces but follows earnings

Step 13. Every day, the order book

If a share is a slice of a business, why does its price jump around every single day while the business barely changes?

You already know the answer from Trading Week 1. The price is only the last trade. Each day it is pushed around by who happens to be in a hurry, by news, by rumors, by big investors adjusting what they hold, and by moods. None of that changes how many tubes of toothpaste a company sells this month.

So over days and weeks, most of what you see on a stock chart is noise, movement that tells you almost nothing about the business underneath.

Step 14. The price to earnings ratio

To connect price and business, compare them directly. Divide the share price by the earnings per share. The result is the price to earnings ratio, written P/E. P/E is also called the multiple, because it says how many times the earnings the market pays.

A share at $60 with EPS of $3 has a P/E of 20. One way to read it is that the market is charging 20 years of this year's earnings for a slice of the company. A P/E of 10 means it is charging 10 years' worth.

Flip the definition around and you get the most useful sentence in this chapter. Price = EPS × P/E. The price can change for only two reasons. Either the earnings change, or the multiple the market is willing to pay for them changes.

This is a way of looking, not a law

P/E is a definition, so Price = EPS × P/E is always true. What is a model is the idea that the multiple drifts around a middle value over decades while earnings do the real climbing. That has broadly held for large US companies over the past century, but multiples have stayed high or low for ten years or more, and nothing guarantees the future looks like the past.

Step 15. Your turn. Twenty days against thirty years

Below are two views of one made up company. The gold line is the share price. The dashed green line is what the price would be if the market always paid the same multiple of earnings. Start on twenty days, then flip to thirty years, and compare how much of the gold line the green line explains.

Over a month, the company's earnings do not change at all, so the green line is flat. The price still zigzags up and down every day. Almost all of that is the order book from Trading Week 1, orders arriving and leaving, not a change in the business.

Over thirty years the earnings grow about 7% a year, and the green line, earnings times a steady multiple of 19, climbs with them. The gold price swings above and below it, sometimes for years. This made up company's multiple was built to wander around 19 and come back. That is the model from the aside above. Real multiples have mostly behaved this way over the last century, but nothing forces them to.

Here is what just happened. Over twenty days this year's earnings did not change, so all of the movement was the multiple. The multiple is the market's running summary of everything it expects. How fast earnings will grow, how risky they are, what interest rates are, and also plain mood. Over a few weeks almost none of that changes much, so most of the wiggle is noise from the order book.

Over thirty years the earnings grew more than sevenfold, and that growth carried the price with it. The swings in the multiple were still there, as the gaps between the lines show. But they stayed within the same range in percent, while the earnings kept multiplying.

This is the core reason long term investing can work. If you hold for decades, what you mostly collect is the growth in the business. If you hold for minutes, what you mostly collect is the noise, and the noise is someone else's gain or loss.

Quick quiz

A share trades at $45 and the company's EPS is $3. What is the P/E?

P/E is price divided by EPS. $45 ÷ $3 = 15. The market is charging 15 years of this year's earnings.

Over ten years a company's EPS doubles and its P/E stays at 18. What happened to the share price?

Price = EPS × P/E. If EPS doubles and P/E is unchanged, price doubles too. Earnings growth with a steady multiple is the whole engine.

Over the same ten years another company's EPS doubles, but its P/E falls from 30 to 15. What happened to its share price?

Price = EPS × P/E. EPS went up by a factor of 2 and the P/E went down by a factor of 2, so the two cancel. The business doubled its profits and the owners saw no price gain at all, because they started out paying a very high multiple.

Why is most of the movement in a stock over a single week poor evidence about the business?

Over short periods the business is nearly fixed, so the price is driven by orders and by changes in the multiple. Over decades the link to earnings shows up, which is why "random at any time scale" is wrong. Most large US companies report earnings every quarter, not once a year.

Interest rates rise sharply and investors decide future profits are worth less today. Earnings have not changed. What most likely happens?

The multiple sums up what the market expects, including interest rates. Higher rates make future profits worth less today, so investors pay a lower multiple. Price = EPS × P/E, so the price falls even though this year's earnings did not.

Chapter 5 Time is the main ingredient

Step 16. Growth on growth

Ronald Read did not just collect his companies' profits. He fed them back in. To see why that matters so much, start with $1,000 that grows 10% a year.

After one year you have $1,100. The second year, the 10% is figured on $1,100, not on the original $1,000, so you gain $110 and end at $1,210. The extra $10 is growth on last year's growth. Growth that builds on earlier growth like this is called compounding.

The picture shows ten years of it. In each bar, the pale part is the $1,000 you started with. The outlined part is the plain 10% of that $1,000, a steady $100 a year. The amber part is everything extra that came from growth on growth.

Look at how the amber part behaves. In year 1 it is zero. By year 10 it is $594, more than a third of all the growth so far. From year 15 on it is more than half. Carry the same picture to year 30 and it is $13,449 of the $16,449 gained, about four fifths. The longer the money sits, the more of the gain is growth on growth. That is why time matters more than almost anything else in this chapter.

Step 17. What stocks have actually returned

To use compounding you need a growth rate, and a made up 10% proves nothing. So here is the real record.

A professor at New York University, Aswath Damodaran, keeps a widely used table of US stock returns going back to 1928. It measures the S&P 500, an index of large US companies that Chapter 6 explains, with all dividends reinvested. Price change plus dividends is called the total return, and every figure in this chapter is a total return.

From 1928 through 2025 the total return was about 10.0% a year, compounded. That is the compounded average, the one steady rate that turns $1 into $11,600 over 98 years. If you simply add up the 98 yearly returns and divide by 98, you get a higher number, about 11.9%, because a loss hurts more than an equal gain helps. Lose 50% and you need a 100% gain to get back. For growth over time, the compounded average is the one that matters.

But a 2025 dollar buys far less than a 1928 dollar. The general rise in prices over time is called inflation, and over this period it averaged about 3% a year. Taking inflation out is a division, not a subtraction. Each year a dollar grew to $1.100 while prices grew to $1.030, so buying power grew 1.100 ÷ 1.030 = 1.068 times, or about 6.8% a year. Subtracting, 10 − 3 = 7, is a quick estimate that is close when the rates are small. The after inflation figure is called the real return. The 10% is the nominal return, counted in dollars of the day.

History is not a promise

These are measurements of what happened in one country over one century, a century in which the US economy grew enormously. The S&P 500 itself began in 1957, and the earlier years use the S&P's smaller index of about 90 large companies. It is the best evidence there is, and it is still no guarantee of the next fifty years.

Step 18. Never smooth

A 10% average sounds calm. The years it is made of were not. Each thin bar below is one calendar year. Bars above the middle line were gains and bars below it were losses. The dashed line is the 10% compounded average.

Count the bars below the line. In 26 of those 98 years stocks lost money, more than one year in four. In 1931 they fell about 44%. In 2008, the year Lehman Brothers failed, they fell about 37%. And calendar years hide the worst runs. The four years 1929 through 1932, one after another, turned $1 into about 35 cents.

The 10% average is built out of all of these bars. Investing Week 3 is about what years like those feel like and how people survive them.

Step 19. The rule of 72

There is a quick way to feel what a growth rate means. Divide 72 by the yearly percentage. The answer is roughly how many years it takes for money to double. This shortcut is called the rule of 72.

At 10% a year, money doubles in about 72 ÷ 10 = 7.2 years. The exact answer is 7.3 years. At 6.8% it doubles in about 72 ÷ 6.8 = 10.6 years, and the exact answer is 10.5. At 2% it takes about 36 years.

Now stack doublings. At 10%, forty years holds about 40 ÷ 7.3, five and a half doublings. 2⁵ is 32 and 2⁶ is 64, and five and a half doublings land between them, at 2⁵·⁵, about 45. That is why a dollar left alone for forty years at 10% becomes about $45.

Why 72?

The exact doubling time is ln 2 ÷ ln(1 + r). For small rates ln(1 + r) is close to r itself, so the doubling time is close to 0.693 ÷ r, which is 69.3 divided by the percentage. But ln(1 + r) is always a little smaller than r, and the gap grows with the rate, so near 8% the number that fits best is about 72. 72 also divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12. Between about 2% and 15% the shortcut is off by less than 4%.

Step 21. What it would have taken

Nobody published how much Ronald Read put in each year or what his exact returns were, so we cannot reconstruct his account. What we can do is check what his result would take.

Suppose someone invests $2,000 at the end of every year for 55 years, from 1959, the year of Read's earliest reported purchase, and earns 10% a year, roughly the long run average. The total put in is $110,000. The ending value is about $3.8 million, and almost all of it is growth on growth. To reach $8 million the same way takes about $4,300 a year.

In 1959 that was a lot of money for a mechanic. Read did spend about $2,380 on a single purchase that year, 39 shares of Pacific Gas and Electric. So either he saved a very large share of what he earned from early on, or his picks did better than the market's average, or both. The Journal called him a good stock picker. Nobody published the numbers that would settle it. What the check does show is that the engine is ordinary. Decades of saving plus growth on growth gets a modest earner into the millions.

Quick quiz

$2,000 grows at 10% a year. How much does it have after two years?

Year one gives $2,000 × 1.10 = $2,200. Year two gives $2,200 × 1.10 = $2,420. The extra $20 over plain growth is growth on the first year's growth.

Using the rule of 72, about how long does money take to double at 8% a year?

72 ÷ 8 = 9. The exact answer is about 9.0 years, so the shortcut is very close at this rate.

An investment returned 9% in a year when inflation was 4%. What was the real return, to one decimal?

Taking inflation out is a division. 1.09 ÷ 1.04 = 1.048, so buying power grew about 4.8%. Subtracting, 9% − 4% = 5%, is the quick estimate, close but not exact. Your money grew 9%, but what it can buy grew only about 4.8%.

Damodaran's table shows two averages for 1928 to 2025, about 10.0% and about 11.9%. Which one tells you how $1 actually grew?

The compounded average is the one steady rate that turns $1 into the actual ending value. The simple average is higher, because a loss hurts more than an equal gain helps. A 50% loss followed by a 50% gain averages 0% but leaves you with 75 cents.

From 1928 through 2025, in how many of the 98 years did US stocks lose money?

Stocks fell in 26 of the 98 years, including drops of about 44% in 1931 and 37% in 2008. A good long run average is built out of many bad years.

Two people each invest $10,000 at the same average return. One reinvests dividends, the other spends them. After 40 years, why does the reinvester have so much more?

The spender's shares only compound at the price growth rate. The reinvester's shares compound at the full return, because every dividend becomes new shares that also grow and pay. Companies treat both owners identically.

Chapter 6 Ninety five baskets

Step 22. The one that failed

Lehman Brothers was a Wall Street bank founded in 1850. Bankruptcy is the legal process for a company that cannot pay its debts. On September 15, 2008 Lehman filed for it, the largest bankruptcy in US history. The people it owed money to were paid what could be recovered, and the owners of its shares, who are paid last, were essentially wiped out. A share that had traded for more than $60 the year before became worth almost nothing.

Ronald Read owned Lehman shares. If they had been all he owned, his fortune would have vanished. Instead he barely noticed, because Lehman was one company out of dozens.

Step 23. Your turn. One basket or ninety five

Flip between the two pictures below. In both, one company fails. The only thing that changes is how many companies the same money was spread across.

Everything you own is in one company. When it fails, its shares become worthless, and so does your whole investment.

The same money spread equally over 95 companies, roughly the number Ronald Read owned. One goes bankrupt and turns gray. The other 94 keep earning. Read's real holdings were not equal sizes, so treat the 1.05% as a picture of the idea, not a measurement of his loss.

Here is what just happened. Owning many companies is called diversification. It works because a single company's disaster, a fraud, a lawsuit, a bad bet, a new competitor, mostly happens to that company alone. Spread across 95 companies, any one failure is a small dent, and the other 94 keep earning profits.

It also means you do not have to be right about every company. Read was wrong about Lehman and still ended with nearly $8 million.

Step 24. Owning the whole list

Diversification taken to its natural end means owning a little of everything. That idea needs two more words.

An index is a list of companies plus a rule for how much of each the list holds. The S&P 500 is the best known. It holds about 500 of the largest US companies, each weighted by its market cap, counting only shares that are available to trade, so a company whose market cap is twice as large counts twice as much. The S&P 500 number you see on the news is calculated from all their share prices.

You have met this before. The ES futures contract in the trading track is a bet on exactly this number. A trader in ES is betting on where the index goes next. An owner of the companies in it is collecting their profits.

A fund is a pool of money from many people that is invested as one, with each person owning a slice of the pool. A fund that simply buys every company in an index, in the index's proportions, is called an index fund. Investing Week 2 is entirely about them, including why most professional stock pickers fail to beat them over long periods.

The other reason to own everything

A study of every US stock from 1926 to 2016 found that the best 4% of companies produced all of the stock market's gain over Treasury bills, the safest short term loans to the US government. The other 96% together did no better than Treasury bills. You cannot know in advance which few will be the winners. Owning all of them is the only way to be sure you hold them.

Step 25. What diversification cannot do

Owning many companies protects you from one company's bad luck. It does not protect you from all of them falling at once. In 1931 and in 2008 nearly every company fell together, because the whole economy was in trouble. A diversified owner fell too, by about the market's 44% and 37%.

So diversification shrinks the risk that comes from any one company. The standard theory in finance says investors are not paid extra for carrying that kind of risk, because anyone can get rid of it almost for free by owning more companies. What is left is the risk of the market as a whole. That is the risk long term owners are paid to sit through, and Investing Week 3 is about it.

Quick quiz

You split $9,500 equally across 95 companies. One goes bankrupt and its shares become worthless. The others are unchanged. How much did you lose?

Each company got $9,500 ÷ 95 = $100. Only that $100 is lost, which is 1 ÷ 95, about 1.05% of the total.

What does diversification protect you from?

Spreading money across many companies shrinks the damage from any one of them. It does nothing when the whole market falls together, and it does not change inflation.

What does the S&P 500 measure?

The S&P 500 weights each company by market cap, its share price times the shares available to trade. A high share price alone does not make a company big, since a company with few shares can have a high price and a small market cap.

A study of US stocks from 1926 to 2016 found that the best 4% of companies produced all of the market's gain over Treasury bills. What does that suggest about owning just a few stocks?

Most of the market's long run gain came from a small group of companies nobody could pick reliably in advance. An owner of a handful of stocks can easily hold none of them. Owning the whole index guarantees holding all of them.

What happens to a company's shareholders when it goes bankrupt and its debts exceed what it owns?

Owners take the leftover after everyone the company owes has been paid. In a bankruptcy like Lehman's there was essentially nothing left over, so the shares became nearly worthless.

Chapter 7 Positive sum

Step 26. Two different games

Now go back to the zero sum idea from Trading Week 1. An ES futures contract is an agreement between two people about a price. Every ES contract has two sides. The trader who agreed to buy is said to be long, and the trader who agreed to sell is said to be short. When the price rises 10 points, the long side gains 10 points and the short side loses exactly 10, so the total for everyone is zero before fees.

A share is different in kind, not just in degree. It is a piece of a business, and the business makes new money every year by selling things people want. That profit did not come out of any other investor's pocket. It came from customers, and it belongs to the owners.

Look at the bottom of each side. On the left the total is zero, every time. On the right the total is the company's profit, year after year, delivered as dividends, buybacks and a growing business. That is why owning companies is called positive sum.

Step 27. Your turn. Profits or a transfer?

Below are six situations. For each one, decide whether money is being created by a business and flowing to its owners, or whether it is only moving from one person to another. The difference is the whole chapter, so take your time on the ones that involve stocks but are not ownership.

Situation 1. You and a friend each take one side of an ES contract and hold it for a year.

A futures contract only moves money between its two sides. Whatever one of you gains over the year, the other loses.

Situation 2. A company pays out part of its yearly profit as dividends.

The cash comes from customers buying the company's products. It goes to every owner and nobody on the other side of a trade loses it.

Situation 3. A day trader buys 100 shares and sells them five minutes later at a higher price.

In five minutes the business earns almost nothing for its owners, so the gain came from whoever sold low or bought high. Over such a short time trading shares is effectively a transfer.

Situation 4. A company reinvests its profit in new stores that earn more than the owners could have earned elsewhere.

The new stores produce new profit that belongs to all the owners. The group as a whole is richer and nobody had to lose.

Situation 5. You buy a share from another investor at a P/E of 30 and sell it a year later at a P/E of 15. Earnings did not change.

The business earned the same either way. You paid twice as much for its earnings as the next buyer did. The investor who sold to you at 30 got a high price, and the one who buys from you at 15 gets the same earnings for half the price. The multiple created no profit. It only decided who paid how much.

Situation 6. A company uses its profit to buy back and cancel some of its shares.

The sellers get cash and the remaining owners get a bigger slice of the same profit. The value delivered comes from the company's earnings.

The traps were the third and fifth. Trading a share is not the same as owning a business. Hold it for five minutes, or profit only from a change in the multiple, and you are playing the transfer game, even though the thing you hold is a share.

Keep these two apart

The profits are positive sum. The price changes that come from the multiple are not. If you buy at a P/E of 30 and sell at 15, you lose half your money even though the business earned the same. The investor who sold to you at 30 got a high price, and the one who buys from you at 15 gets the same earnings for half the price. Owners as a group collect the earnings. Individuals who buy high and sell low can still do badly, and Investing Week 7 is about why so many do.

Step 28. Who paid Ronald Read

Now the one question can be answered.

His money came from the profits of the companies he owned. Every time someone bought Tide, Smucker's jam, a prescription at CVS, or paid the interest on a loan from JPMorgan Chase, a little of the profit was his. The companies sent some of it to him as dividends, which he used to buy more shares. They spent some buying back shares from owners who wanted out, which handed the profit to him as a bigger slice of each company. And they reinvested the rest, which grew the profits that the next decades would deliver.

On top of that he had two things most people do not use. He had time, more than five decades by the Journal's account of a purchase he made in 1959, which let growth on growth do most of the work. And he rarely sold, so he paid very little in trading costs and never handed his gains to someone on the other side of a panic.

So who lost what he won? Nobody had to. That is the difference between owning a business and betting on its next price.

Step 29. Why one game pays owners by default and the other pays almost nobody

Put the two games side by side one last time. In day trading ES, the money you make has to come from another trader, and after fees the whole group loses. The Taiwan study found that fewer than 1 in 100 day traders could beat the market after fees, reliably, year after year.

In long term ownership, the profits of the businesses flow to all the owners by default. You do not need to beat anyone to receive your share of them. You need to own many businesses, ideally a whole index, keep your costs low, and stay owners through the bad years. Most people who do those three things for decades end up close to the market's return for their period, minus their costs, whatever that return turns out to be.

Beating other owners is still zero sum

Owning the market is positive sum. Trying to beat the market is not. Before costs, the stock pickers as a group hold, in effect, the market, so as a group they must earn the market's return. Every dollar one of them earns above it, another earns below it. After costs the group falls behind. Investing Week 2 builds this.

This is a description of how the two games are built, not advice to play one or the other. The trading track exists to find out honestly whether you can be in the 1%. This track exists so that whatever the answer, you understand the game where the odds are built in your favor.

Quick quiz

In one year, ES rises 200 points. Before fees, what is the total gain of all ES contract holders added together?

Each ES contract has a long side and a short side, so gains and losses cancel exactly. Company profits do not flow to futures holders at all. They flow to the owners of the shares.

Where did most of the money in Ronald Read's estate ultimately come from?

He rarely sold and did not trade for a living. His companies' profits reached him as dividends, buybacks and business growth, and decades of reinvesting let them compound.

Why can an individual stock investor still lose money even though ownership is positive sum?

The earnings are positive sum for owners as a group, but the multiple decides how much each buyer pays for them. Pay 30 times earnings and sell at 15, and half your money is gone while the business is unchanged. The other way is simpler. The business itself can shrink or fail, as Lehman did. Positive sum describes all owners together, not any one company.

What is the core difference between a share of stock and an ES futures contract?

The difference is what they are, not how long you hold them. A share entitles its owner to part of a business's profits, held for a day or for decades. A futures contract only moves money between its two sides, at any holding period. Both trade on exchanges.

Chapter 8 On your own

Step 30. One full problem

Work this straight through on paper first, then check each part. Here is a made up company.

ItemValue
Shares outstanding800 million
Last trade price$75
Earnings this year$3.0 billion
Dividend per share, each quarter$0.45

Quick quiz

Part 1. What is the market cap?

800 million shares × $75 = $60,000 million, which is $60 billion. That is what the market is charging for the whole company at the last trade.

Part 2. What is the EPS?

Earnings ÷ shares outstanding = $3.0 billion ÷ 800 million = $3.75 per share.

Part 3. What is the P/E?

Price ÷ EPS = $75 ÷ $3.75 = 20. The market is charging 20 years of this year's earnings.

Part 4. What is the dividend yield?

The yearly dividend is 4 × $0.45 = $1.80. $1.80 ÷ $75 = 0.024, which is 2.4%.

Part 5. The company spends $3.75 billion of its cash to buy back and cancel 50 million shares at $75 each. Earnings stay at $3.0 billion. What is the new EPS?

800 million − 50 million = 750 million shares remain. $3.0 billion ÷ 750 million = $4.00 per share. The $3.53 answer added the 50 million shares instead of removing them.

Part 6. EPS rose to $4.00. Does the share price rise to $4.00 × 20 = $80?

Before the buyback the market charged $60 billion for the whole company, cash included. $3.75 billion of that cash went to the sellers, which leaves about $56.25 billion for 750 million shares, or $75 each. EPS went up, so the P/E fell to $75 ÷ $4.00 = 18.75. The owners who stayed are no richer and no poorer. Their gain came from the profit the company earned, not from the buyback.

Part 7. An owner puts $5,000 into this company's shares and the total return, with dividends reinvested, averages 7% a year. Using the rule of 72, roughly what is it worth after about 20 years?

At 7% money doubles in about 72 ÷ 7, roughly 10 years. Twenty years is two doublings, so $5,000 becomes about $20,000. The exact figure is $5,000 × 1.07²⁰, about $19,350.

Step 31. Mixed questions, the way the exam asks them

The weekly exam mixes everything, so this last set does too.

Quick quiz

A company's EPS rises 10% this year but it earned exactly the same total profit as last year. What most likely happened?

Same profit with higher EPS means fewer shares. If shares fall by a fraction f, EPS rises by 1 ÷ (1 − f). A 10% rise needs f of about 9%. The price and the dividend do not enter into EPS.

Which change would raise a share's price if nothing else changed?

Price = EPS × P/E, so higher EPS from growing profit, with the same P/E, means a higher price. A falling P/E lowers it, and paying a dividend moves cash out of the company, so the price typically drops by about that amount.

An investment grows 8% a year for 36 years. By the rule of 72, roughly how many times larger is it?

At 8% money doubles about every 9 years. 36 years is four doublings, and 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16. The exact figure is 1.08³⁶, about 16.0.

In the calculator, $1,000 at 6.8% for 30 years ends at $7,197 with dividends reinvested. With dividends spent, the shares end at $3,061 and $1,627 of dividends was spent along the way. How much more did reinvesting leave you, counting the spent cash?

Spent, you have $3,061 + $1,627 = $4,688 in total. Reinvested, you have $7,197. The difference is $7,197 − $4,688 = $2,509. The $4,136 answer forgot the dividends you did receive.

Company A's shares trade at $500 and it has 10 million shares. Company B's shares trade at $50 and it has 1 billion shares. Which counts for more in an index weighted by market cap?

Market cap is price times shares. A is $500 × 10 million = $5 billion. B is $50 × 1 billion = $50 billion. B counts ten times as much. Share price alone tells you nothing about a company's size.

Which of these best describes Ronald Read's approach, according to the reporting?

The Wall Street Journal reported at least 95 holdings, many held for decades, an avoidance of technology stocks, and reinvested dividends. He was patient, diversified and cheap to run.

Looking at any company you might own

  2. Find the market cap and remember it is a price, not a value. Step 6
  3. Find the EPS and how the company delivers it, as dividends, buybacks or reinvestment. Step 9
  4. Compute the P/E and ask how much of the price is earnings and how much is the multiple. Step 14
  5. Think in decades and real returns, and use the rule of 72 to feel what a rate means. Step 19

The whole story in one breath

A share is a fraction of a real business, and the business's profits belong to its owners, who are paid last and can never lose more than they put in, and who receive those profits as dividends, as buybacks that enlarge their slice, and as a business that grows. Day to day the price is pushed around by the order book and by changes in the multiple, but over decades it follows the earnings. Ronald Read owned dozens of ordinary companies for most of his life, reinvested what they paid him, and let more than five decades of growth on growth turn a mechanic's and janitor's savings into nearly $8 million, and because those profits came from customers rather than from other traders, nobody had to lose for him to win.
